Written for the facilitator who can't leave the room

Most group-therapy books explain group dynamics. This one is for the minute after something goes wrong, when you are alone with eight members and a lesson plan.

Three ways to say it
Every scenario opens with three alternative first responses in plain spoken language, so you can pick the one that sounds like you.
Branches, not a script
What to do if the member responds, stays quiet, escalates, or something else is going on, including when to stop teaching altogether.
A way back to the skill
Each scenario returns to a named DBT skill, so the session still delivers what it was planned to deliver.
Solo and co-facilitator routes
What changes when you have a second staff member, and what you must never do when you don't, such as leaving the group unattended.
Documentation excerpts
Alternative note excerpts for different outcomes, recording observed behavior, interventions and stated responses, without other members' details.
One-page quick-reference cards

The 39 situations

Grouped the way the book's index is grouped, so you can find the decision in front of you in seconds.

Participation and access

  • Nobody answers the opening question
  • A member cannot use the written worksheet
  • The room goes quiet after a painful disclosure
  • The available language support does not meet the task

Time and speaking turns

  • One member needs more time than the group can offer
  • A member repeatedly interrupts other speakers
  • Advice takes over another member's turn
  • Check-in leaves too little time for practice

Conflict and impact

  • A disagreement becomes personal
  • A comment targets a member's identity
  • Laughter interrupts a member's contribution
  • Several members question whether a boundary is fair

Task boundaries

  • A member declines the activity
  • Members ask for graphic details of a painful event
  • A member asks for diagnosis or medication advice
  • A member asks for a guarantee that the skill will work

Membership changes

  • A new member joins midway through the cycle
  • Returning members say they have done this skill already
  • Arrivals and departures interrupt the session
  • A familiar member is unexpectedly absent

Energy and practical fit

  • A tired member struggles to stay with the lesson
  • A practice exercise increases distress
  • The room becomes difficult to tolerate
  • A suggested skill does not fit a real constraint

The facilitator relationship

  • The facilitator makes a harmful assumption
  • A member raises a legitimate concern about the intervention
  • A member points out an inconsistent boundary
  • Co-facilitators give conflicting directions

Follow through and transitions

  • A member leaves the room during tension
  • A member returns after individual support
  • The same disruption recurs across sessions
  • The session must end while tension remains

When safety is at risk

  • A member says they plan to end their life
  • A member appears intoxicated
  • A member threatens another member

Virtual groups

  • A virtual member hints at danger, then disconnects
  • Someone else is in a virtual member's room
  • A member joins while driving
  • A private chat message asks you to keep a secret

Acute moments and video groups

Scenarios 33 to 35 cover a member who says they plan to end their life, a member who appears intoxicated, and a threat toward another member. Scenarios 36 to 39 cover groups held by video, including a member who hints at danger and then disconnects. These scenarios show a facilitator's first response: what to say, how to stay with the group, and how to hand off. They are not a suicide risk assessment, crisis protocol or security procedure, and they point you to your program's emergency procedures every time.

Who it is for

Group facilitators in adult intensive outpatient (IOP) and partial hospitalization (PHP) programs, clinical trainees and interns who lead or co-lead skills groups, and supervisors who want a shared language for reviewing hard sessions. It works alongside any skills curriculum, including our Open-Enrollment Program Series.

What it is not

The cases are fictional illustrations. The worked skills come from dialectical behavior therapy, but the book is not a DBT curriculum or DBT training. It does not replace supervision, individual assessment or your program's procedures, and the documentation excerpts are not complete notes or guarantees of reimbursement.
