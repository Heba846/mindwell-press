How to run a rolling-admission therapy group

By Heba El Garhy · Published October 19, 2026 · Updated November 2, 2026 · Editorial standards

In a rolling-admission program, today's group might hold a member on their first day, three in their second week, and one stepping down on Thursday. This guide covers how to run a session that works for all of them, without re-teaching the basics every time or leaving the newest member lost.

What rolling admission means for a group

Rolling admission (also called open enrollment or continuous entry) means a program admits people when they need care rather than waiting to fill a cohort. Federal treatment guidance for intensive outpatient care lists easing entry as a core principle and says programs should aim to make the first appointment available on demand. Programs that kept their groups open to new members have reported shorter waits between assessment and treatment, according to case examples collected by NIATX at the University of Wisconsin.

The trade-off lands on the facilitator. Any given group holds people at very different points in treatment on the same day.

Why cohort curricula break

Most group curricula are written for a cohort that starts in week 1 and finishes in week 12 together. Run one under rolling admission and three things tend to happen:

  1. You re-teach the basics every time someone joins, and members who have covered them lose interest.
  2. You skip ahead to keep pace with the printed schedule, and new members are lost.
  3. You improvise, because no single printed session fits a day-one and a day-thirty member.

None of these is a facilitator failure. They are the predictable result of using a cohort-shaped tool for a rolling-admission job.

Run a rotation, not a sequence

A sequence has a fixed order that assumes everyone starts together. A rotation is a cycle that repeats, and a member can join it on any turn, like a book club that discusses whatever chapter is next rather than requiring everyone to start the book on the same day. Eventually the group cycles back to what a member missed.

For a rotation to work, no session can depend on an earlier one. Each session needs its own short orientation, its own teaching points and its own practice, so it delivers something complete to anyone in the room.

Open every session with a 3-minute Newcomer Bridge

The Newcomer Bridge is a short scripted opening that orients someone in the room for the first time and re-grounds everyone else before the session builds on the idea. It has three parts:

  1. Name the session's one idea in a sentence. "Today we're looking at how to check whether an emotion fits the facts of a situation."
  2. Ask a 60-second recall question of the group generally. "For anyone who's done this before: in one sentence, what's this skill for?" Anyone can answer, and no one has to.
  3. State the ground rule. What will happen in the session, and that any prompt can be passed.

"Welcome, everyone. If this is your first group with us, welcome. You don't need to have been here before for any of this to be useful today. Today we're working on [skill]. [One-sentence description.] If you've worked on this before, feel free to share in a sentence what it's about for you. And as always, any question or activity today, you're welcome to pass."

Run the Bridge every time, even when everyone in the room has done the session before. It costs three minutes and lets the rest of the session work for anyone, whenever they started. It does not replace a proper first-day orientation (see below).

Ask two levels of question in one room

A mixed-tenure group holds people meeting an idea for the first time next to people meeting it for the second or third time. Write two tiers of processing question for every session:

  • First time: questions about understanding and noticing. "Where might this skill have helped you this week?"
  • Returning: questions about applying it now, or explaining it in their own words. "You've worked with this skill before. What has it looked like for you this week?"

You rarely need to track who gets which tier. Open a question to the room and the answers usually sort themselves: newer members answer at the first-time level, and returning members often go further on their own. When that doesn't happen, direct a returning question to someone by name, without ranking members by experience.

Some programs invite, but never assign, a returning member to demonstrate an example or pair with a newer member during practice. The member can decline without explanation, and the facilitator stays responsible for the group throughout.

When returning members say "we've done this already"

This is the most predictable friction point in an open group, and the newest member often goes quiet while it happens. A first response that takes the concern seriously without defending the schedule:

"You've heard this before, and you want the time to be useful. I'll explain what is repeated and what can be different."

Then follow what actually happens. If a member wants more challenge, offer a harder application of the same skill rather than extra busywork. If someone starts instructing peers, return to one speaker at a time. If someone needs the basics, keep the explanation available without making them disclose personal history. And if anything suggests urgent harm or a clinical change, pause the lesson for your program's assessment or emergency procedure; do not finish the task first.

Orientation and tracking

  • Orient before the first group. A Bridge is not orientation. Each new member should get a short individual or small-group orientation covering program structure, group norms and crisis resources before or at their first group, not squeezed into the first three minutes of a session. See how to orient a new member to an open therapy group.
  • Let members track their own progress. A simple client-held record of completed sessions shows the member what they have covered and shows any facilitator, at a glance, what they haven't. It helps when staff rotate or attendance is irregular.
  • Plan for leaving mid-rotation. Most members step down before completing a full cycle. Use their record at step-down to give short catch-up pages on what they missed, so nothing is simply dropped. The next section covers the step-down itself.

How do I handle a member stepping down mid-rotation?

Treat a step-down as a change in how often the member attends, not as a graduation. The rotation keeps turning: the member leaves on whatever session they are on, takes a record of what they covered, and the group is told only what it needs in order to run the next session.

What changes for the member when their hours drop?

Their schedule changes; the group's format does not. A step-down means fewer hours and usually fewer groups per day, and the member will meet each skill less often. How many hours each level involves is covered in PHP vs IOP group therapy: what changes for the facilitator. A Medicare local coverage determination for psychiatric partial hospitalization names "stepping down to a less intensive level of outpatient care" as one of the ways a patient leaves a PHP, so a step-down is an ordinary part of a PHP stay.

PHP to IOPFor the member: shorter days, fewer groups, more of the day outside the program. For you: check their record for the sessions they will no longer reach each week and prioritise those for catch-up pages.
IOP to weekly or less frequent groupFor the member: one group a week means meeting each skill far less often. For you: make the Newcomer Bridge's recall question count, because a weekly member may not have seen the last skill for a month.
Leaving the programFor the member: no more sessions. For you: hand over the record and catch-up pages, and follow your program's discharge and aftercare process.

Who tells the group, and what do they say?

The facilitator tells the group, briefly, and only what affects the room: that a member's attendance days are changing. Do not explain the reason, the level of care or the treatment team's decision. Whether to say anything at all depends on your program's policy, and the member should hear it from you first and choose whether they want to say more themselves.

"Before we start: [Name] will be with us on Tuesdays and Thursdays from next week. [Name], is there anything you'd like to say, or would you rather we just keep going?"

The norms about privacy that members hear at orientation (see how to orient a new member to an open therapy group) are what make a one-sentence update safe to give. Tell the member privately beforehand. The treatment team, not the group facilitator, decides and communicates the clinical change; the facilitator's job is to make sure the member is not surprised in front of peers.

Does the member say goodbye, or keep attending?

It depends on whether they are leaving the group or only attending it less. A member who is stepping down to fewer days is still in the group, so a farewell can send the wrong message. A member who is leaving the group entirely may want a closing moment.

Offer a short, optional closing round either way, with a pass always available. Ready-to-read closings of this kind, including new-member and telehealth versions, are what Group Openers and Closers collects. Examples:

  • "One thing you're taking with you from this group."
  • "One skill you want to keep practising on the days you're not here."
  • "Something you'd tell the next person who joins."

Avoid asking other members to give feedback to the member who is leaving; it puts the member on display and can pull the session away from its topic.

How does the rotation absorb a step-down without a graduation ritual?

Because every session is complete in itself, nobody is mid-way through anything when they step down. A cohort curriculum ends with a final session that members are expected to reach together, and a step-down mid-cycle leaves a hole in that structure. A rotation has no final session to miss, so the member simply stops on the turn they are on and the cycle continues.

What replaces the graduation is the record. Use the member's own list of completed sessions to hand over short catch-up pages for the sessions they have not reached. Those pages are the continuity, and they cost less than a ritual that the rest of the group has no way to share.

What if the step-down is a payer or authorization decision?

Keep the facilitator's role the same: do not explain or defend the decision in group. In some programs a change in level of care follows an authorization or utilization-review decision rather than the team's clinical view alone, and how that is communicated varies by program. Send questions about it to the treatment team, case manager or utilization-review staff.

The same Medicare local coverage determination says coverage for partial hospitalization requires a "reasonable expectation of improvement" and that active treatment directly address the problems that led to admission. Those are criteria for the team and the payer to apply. A facilitator who is asked "why am I stepping down?" can say honestly that this belongs with the treatment team, then hold the same space for the member that day as any other.

How do I document the transition?

Record what you observed and delivered, as in any group note, and add that the member's attendance schedule is changing and what you handed over. Do not record the payer's reasoning or other members' reactions in that member's note. The Medicare determination cited above says absences from a partial hospitalization program, and their cause, must be documented in the medical record, so show that reduced attendance after a step-down is scheduled rather than unexplained.

Wording and format vary by program and payer, so follow your program's documentation policy. See Group therapy progress notes: GIRP and DAP examples for note structure.

Worked example (invented)

This is an invented example for illustration; it does not describe a real person. Dana is in the Wednesday group and is moving from a full PHP schedule to three IOP days. The treatment team tells Dana on Monday. Before Wednesday's group the facilitator confirms with Dana that she is comfortable with the group knowing her days are changing, and tells the room in one sentence at the start of the session.

After the Bridge, the group runs its normal session. In the closing round Dana says one skill she wants to keep practising; she is welcome to pass. The facilitator gives her catch-up pages for the two sessions in her record that she is least likely to reach on her new days, and writes in her note that her schedule changed, what was handed over, and her stated response.

Documenting a mixed-tenure group

Record what you observed and delivered: the skill taught, the practice offered, and each member's stated or observed response. Familiarity is not mastery. A returning member who says they know the skill has reported familiarity; record practice only when it was demonstrated or reported, and say which. Keep other members' identities and disclosures out of an individual's note, and adapt all wording to your program's requirements and payer rules.

What the rotation does not decide

A rotation decides which topic is taught when. It does not decide who is ready for group, what level of care someone needs or when they should step down. Those are clinical decisions for the treatment team, made through the program's own assessment and treatment-planning process.

Common questions

What is a rolling-admission therapy group?

Rolling admission, also called open enrollment or continuous entry, means a program admits people when they need care rather than waiting to fill a cohort. Any given group holds people at very different points in treatment on the same day, so the facilitator has to run sessions that work for a first-day member and a returning member together.

How do I run a group when new members join every week?

Run a rotation, not a sequence. A rotation is a cycle that repeats, so a member can join on any turn. Make each session complete, with its own short orientation, teaching points and practice, and open every session with a three-minute Newcomer Bridge that names the one idea, asks a recall question and states the ground rule.

How do I respond when returning members say they have done a session already?

Take the concern seriously without defending the schedule. One response is: You've heard this before, and you want the time to be useful. I'll explain what is repeated and what can be different. If a member wants more challenge, offer a harder application of the same skill rather than extra busywork. If anything suggests urgent harm, pause the lesson for your program's procedure.

How do I ask questions that work for both new and returning members?

Write two tiers of processing question for every session. First-time questions focus on understanding and noticing, such as where a skill might have helped this week. Returning questions focus on applying it now or explaining it in their own words. Open a question to the room and answers usually sort themselves; otherwise direct a returning question to someone by name.

How do I handle a member stepping down from PHP to IOP in an open group?

Treat it as a change of schedule, not an ending. The member stays in the rotation and keeps their own session record. Tell the group only which days the member will now attend, never why. Hand over catch-up pages for sessions they will miss, confirm which groups they now join, and document the transition according to your program's policy.

Should a member who is stepping down say goodbye to the group?

Only if they are actually leaving the group. A member moving from five days to two is still in the room on their remaining days, so a farewell can feel premature. Offer an optional one-sentence closing round, such as one thing they are taking with them, and let them pass. Follow your program's policy on discharge and transition.
