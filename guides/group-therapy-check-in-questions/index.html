Group therapy check-in questions for adults: 60 prompts for new and returning members

By Heba El Garhy · Published October 22, 2026 · Editorial standards

A good check-in tells you who is in the room today and gets everyone into the topic in under ten minutes. A bad one runs long, rewards the most talkative member, or leaves a first-day member with nothing to say. Here are 60 check-in and check-out prompts for adult IOP, PHP and outpatient groups, sorted by what you need them to do.

Five rules that make any check-in work

  1. One prompt at a time. "How are you, and how did your practice go?" is two questions, and people answer the easier one.
  2. Don't assume everyone was here last time. In an open group, a question that depends on the last session leaves new members out. Ask about today or the past few days instead.
  3. Offer more than one way to answer. Speak, give a number, write it privately, point, or pass. Say this before the first answer, not after a silence.
  4. No details of painful events. A check-in invites a headline, not a story. Say so, and gently redirect if someone starts going into detail.
  5. Set the time and model it. Answer first yourself, briefly, at the length you want. With eight members, 30 to 60 seconds each keeps check-in to about ten minutes.

Quick check-ins: scales and one-word answers

Use these when time is short, energy is low, or the group is large. They work for everyone, including someone on day one.

  1. On a scale of 0 to 10, how much energy do you have for group today?
  2. One word for how you're arriving.
  3. If today were weather, what would it be?
  4. Thumbs up, sideways, or down for the last 24 hours.
  5. What's one thing you can see in this room right now?
  6. How settled do you feel, 0 to 10? You can keep the number to yourself.
  7. What's taking up the most space in your head right now, in one or two words?
  8. Pick a color for this morning.

For someone on their first day

These need no history with the group. Ask them of everyone, so the new member isn't singled out.

  1. What would you like the group to call you?
  2. What's one thing that helped you get here today?
  3. What would make the next hour a little more useful for you?
  4. What's one thing you'd want a new person to know about how you like to take part? Talking, listening, or a bit of both?
  5. On a scale of 0 to 10, how comfortable are you speaking in a group? Any number is fine.
  6. What's one thing you're curious about today?

For returning members

These ask people to apply something, not to recite it. They also give returning members a role that helps newer ones.

  1. What's one skill from group you used, or meant to use, since you were last here?
  2. Where did a skill not work the way you hoped?
  3. What would you tell someone on their first day about this group?
  4. What's different about how you handled something this week, even a little?
  5. What's one thing you're still practicing?
  6. What's one small step you took that nobody else would notice?

Linked to the skill you're teaching

The best check-in leads straight into the topic. Pick the one that matches today's session.

  • Mindfulness: (21) Name one thing you notice in your body right now, without deciding if it's good or bad. (22) Describe one ordinary thing you saw on the way here, using only what a camera would capture.
  • Emotion regulation: (23) What emotion has been around most since yesterday? (24) What's one routine, like sleep, food or movement, that's helping or getting in the way this week?
  • Distress tolerance: (25) What's one thing that helped you get through a hard moment recently, even partly? (26) What's one comfort you have access to today?
  • Thoughts (CBT): (27) Is there a thought that keeps coming back this week? You don't have to say what it is, just whether it's loud or quiet. (28) Did a thought ever turn out to be less true than it felt at the time?
  • Behavioral activation: (29) What's one activity, however small, you did this week that you're glad you did? (30) What's one thing you've been putting off?
  • Boundaries and requests: (31) Was there a moment this week you wanted to say no, or ask for something? (32) What's easier for you: asking for help or saying no?

For process groups

  1. What are you bringing into the room today?
  2. What's one thing you'd like the group to know about where you are right now?
  3. Is there anything from last time you'd like to come back to? You can say "no".
  4. What do you need from the group today: to talk, to listen, or something else?
  5. What's one feeling you noticed as you walked in?
  6. What would make it easier to say something real today?
  7. What's been hardest about this week, in one sentence?
  8. What's something you're hoping for, even if you're not sure it will happen?

For low-energy or hard days

For days when the room is heavy, after a difficult event in the program, or near a holiday.

  1. You can pass on this one: what's one thing that's OK right now?
  2. What's one thing your body needs in the next hour?
  3. On a scale of 0 to 10, how hard was it to come today?
  4. Who or what is helping you hold things together right now? A person, a pet, a routine, anything.
  5. What's the smallest thing that would make today 1% better?

For video groups

On video, give a clear order and a non-verbal option, like the chat or a hand signal, for people who can't unmute.

  1. Show or name one object within arm's reach.
  2. Type one word in the chat for how you're arriving.
  3. Hold up 0 to 5 fingers for your energy level.
  4. Is where you're sitting private enough for today's group? A yes or no in the chat is enough.

Check-out questions to close the group

A short check-out helps people leave on purpose, and helps you notice who may need a private word before they go.

  1. One word for how you're leaving.
  2. What's one thing you're taking with you from today?
  3. What's one small thing you'll try before the next group?
  4. On a scale of 0 to 10, how settled do you feel now, compared with when you arrived?
  5. What's one thing you noticed about someone else's idea that might help you? No names needed.
  6. Who or what will you turn to if tonight gets hard?
  7. What's one thing you'd like to remember from this session?
  8. What will you do in the next hour to look after yourself?
  9. What's one question you're leaving with?
  10. Is there anything you need from staff before you go? You can tell me privately.
  11. What made today's group easier or harder for you to take part in?

When the check-in goes wrong

Nobody answers. Check the question before reading anything into the silence: it may be two questions in one, or assume history the room doesn't share. Model an answer and offer a smaller choice. The full walkthrough is in What to do when nobody talks in group therapy.

One person takes ten minutes. Acknowledge what they said, name the time ("I want to make sure everyone gets a turn; can we come back to this?"), and offer a specific later moment or a private check-in. Setting the time limit before the first answer makes this much easier.

Returning members say "we've done this already". Switch to a returning-member prompt (15 to 20 above) and ask them to apply it to something current, or to help a newer member.

A check-in reveals possible risk. If an answer suggests someone may be in danger, stop the round and follow your program's procedure. A check-in prompt is not a risk assessment, and the response is never "let's come back to that after group". In the US, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available 24/7 by call, text or chat (988lifeline.org). If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 and follow your program's emergency procedure.
