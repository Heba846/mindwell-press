Telehealth group therapy activities for virtual IOP and PHP: check-ins, grounding and closings that work on video

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 13, 2026 · Editorial standards

Telehealth group therapy activities for virtual IOP and PHP work best when they don't depend on seeing faces. Use a fixed speaking order, check-ins members can answer in chat, eyes-open grounding that works in any room, a plan for members who aren't alone, and a closing round that ends the call on purpose instead of with a sudden drop.

The examples below come from an imagined adult virtual IOP group with rolling admission. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional. These activities are facilitation ideas, not evidence-based interventions. Programs vary, so follow your program's policy on cameras, platforms, privacy and emergencies.

What changes when a group moves to video?

On video, you lose most of the room. You can't see who is about to speak, who has gone quiet, or who else is nearby. Activities that rely on eye contact, passing an object or reading the room need a spoken or typed version.

Some things get easier. Members who would never speak first will often type. A chat line can hold an answer that a member isn't ready to say aloud. Members who can't travel can still attend.

In the roomYou see who wants to talk. Turn-taking happens with a glance. Privacy is set by the building. The session ends when people walk out together.
On videoYou see faces, or black tiles. Turn-taking needs a stated order. Privacy depends on where each member is sitting. The session ends when someone clicks a button.

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services' best-practice guide on group teletherapy starts in the same place. It advises facilitators to "make clear ground rules covering what is acceptable and what is not." Its examples include cameras on, joining from a room where the member can be alone, and using the raise-hand feature to speak. For the full set of video rules in member wording, see group therapy ground rules for telehealth.

The rest of this guide covers the activities that sit on top of those rules: how turns go, how check-ins run, how grounding starts and how the call ends.

How do you set a speaking order on video?

Say the order out loud and post it in chat before the first round. On video, "whoever wants to go first" usually means a long silence and then two people talking at once. A stated order removes the guessing.

Don't use "go in the order of your screen." The tiles can sit in a different order on each member's screen, so nobody knows who is next. Use something everyone can see the same way:

  • The posted list. Type the first names in chat in the order you want. Members can scroll up to see when they're on.
  • The hand-off. Each member names the next person when they finish. It keeps people listening. Give newer members the option to hand back to you.
  • Raise hand, then order. For open discussion, members use the raise-hand button and you call names in the order hands went up.

Build passing into the order. HHS suggests letting participants know "that they can always decide to pass if they do not want to talk when they are called upon." On video, make the pass quick: a member can say "pass" or type it.

Pause after each turn. HHS also advises facilitators to "pause so that everyone has a chance to contribute." Audio delay means a member may start speaking a second after you've moved on. A three-second gap fixes most of that.

Fictional example. Facilitator Renée posts "Order today: Ana, Dev, Lou, Kim, Sal, then me" in chat. She says, "If you'd rather pass, just type pass. Ana, whenever you're ready." When Dev's audio cuts out, she says, "Dev, we'll come back to you. Lou, you're next." Nobody has to wonder what happens now.

What check-ins work in chat?

The best chat check-ins are short, need no explanation and can be answered in under ten seconds. They work for members with cameras off, members in shared spaces and members who aren't ready to speak. Read the answers aloud in general terms, without naming who said what, unless members choose to add more.

  1. The number. "Type a number from 1 to 10 for your energy right now." It's quick, and you can see the room's level at a glance.
  2. The chat waterfall. Everyone types a one-word answer but doesn't hit enter until you say "go." All the answers land at once, so nobody copies the first reply.
  3. The weather report. "Type the weather that matches your morning: sunny, cloudy, foggy or stormy." It lets members say something without explaining it.
  4. A, B or C. Give three options, such as "A: I want to talk today. B: I want to listen. C: somewhere in between." It tells you who to invite in and who to leave alone.
  5. The skill tag. "Type the name of one skill you used since last group, or 'none yet.'" It links the check-in to the work without asking for a story.
  6. One thing on your desk. "Type one object you can see from where you're sitting." It brings attention into the room, and it works on a hard day.
  7. Finish the sentence. "Today I'm hoping group helps with..." Members type the ending in a few words. Newer members can type "not sure yet."
  8. The emoji pick. "React with the emoji that fits how you're arriving." Give a short list so it doesn't turn into a joke round.

Tell members how private the chat is before the first round. Messages to everyone are seen by everyone, and your platform's settings decide what is saved. If your program allows private messages to the facilitator, say so, and say what you will do with them.

What grounding starts work for members joining from anywhere?

Use eyes-open exercises that ask members to notice their own space, need no props and always offer a non-body option. Members may be in a bedroom, a parked car or a kitchen with family nearby. A good video grounding start works in all of those places and takes under two minutes.

  1. Three colors. "Look around and find three things in your space that are blue. Type one if you like." Change the color each week.
  2. The farthest point. "Look away from your screen at the farthest thing you can see. Notice its edges. Now come back to the screen." It gives eyes a break from the call, too.
  3. One sound. "Listen for ten seconds. Name one sound in your space, or type it." In a busy home, the sound might be a TV. That's still an answer.
  4. Feet and seat, or the room. "If it feels okay, notice where your feet meet the floor. If you'd rather not focus on your body, name one thing on the wall instead." Always offer both.
  5. Object in hand. "Pick up one safe, ordinary object near you, like a mug or a pen. Notice its weight and temperature." Members who'd rather not can just look at the object.
  6. The light check. "Where is the light coming from in your space? A window, a lamp, the screen?" It's simple, it needs no disclosure and it orients members to where they are.

Keep eyes open and keep it short. Don't use long breathwork or closed-eye exercises as a default on video, where you can't see who is struggling. For a fuller list and how to choose by the state of the room, see grounding exercises for group therapy.

How do you handle privacy when members aren't alone?

Agree on a quiet signal before you need it, and never ask a member to explain who is with them in front of the group. Many members join from shared homes. A plan set in the first session protects them and protects everyone else's confidentiality.

HHS's guidance on protecting patients' privacy suggests that patients find a private location for the session and that people "use headphones so others do not overhear confidential information." In a group, headphones protect the other members as much as the one wearing them.

A simple plan has four parts:

  • The signal. A member who is no longer private types an agreed word in chat, such as "busy," or sends you a private message if your program allows it. You won't call on them for personal sharing until they say the room is clear.
  • The listening role. A member who can't speak freely can still listen with headphones, answer in chat or type "pass." They're still in group.
  • The pause. If someone walks into a member's room, the member can mute and turn off their camera for a moment without explaining. Say at the start that this is fine.
  • No details aloud. Ask members not to name other members or repeat what was said once the call ends, the same as in the room.

Your program may also ask members to confirm where they are at the start of each session. HHS's emergency planning guidance for telebehavioral health advises providers to "confirm the address where the patient is located at the time of the visit." Do this privately or in the way your procedures describe, not as a round in front of the group. If anything raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures.

Fictional example. Halfway through a skills group, Kim types "busy" in chat. Renée doesn't comment. When Kim's turn comes, she says, "Kim, you're welcome to type a word or pass." Kim types "pass." Ten minutes later Kim types "clear," and Renée invites her back in at the next round.

How do you end a video group without an abrupt drop?

Signal the end early, run a short closing round, and say what happens next before anyone leaves. In the room, a group ends as people stand and walk out together. On video, one click and the room is gone. A planned ending gives members a moment between group and the rest of their day.

  1. The ten-minute flag. "We have ten minutes left. In five, we'll start the closing round." It stops a heavy topic from opening at 11:58.
  2. One word to take with you. Each member says or types one word or one skill they're taking from today. Use the same order as the check-in.
  3. Back to your room. "Before you close the call, look at one thing in your space and name it to yourself." It moves attention from the screen to where the member actually is.
  4. Next session out loud. Say the day and time of the next group and post it in chat. Members who join through a link can lose track of the schedule.
  5. A shared goodbye. Everyone waves or says goodbye at once, then leaves. It feels like walking out together.
  6. The facilitator leaves last. Stay on until the last member has gone, so no one is left alone in an open call.

Plan for dropped connections, too. HHS advises discussing with each patient "what to do if there is a problem with the audio or video connection." For groups, a standing rule helps: if you drop, try to rejoin with the same link. If a member drops during a hard moment and doesn't return, follow your program's procedures.

After a heavy session, use a containment closing rather than a quick one. For more, see the containment section of group therapy closing activities.

Common questions

What are good telehealth group therapy activities for IOP?

Good telehealth group therapy activities for IOP don't depend on reading faces. Use a posted speaking order, check-ins members can answer in chat, such as a 1 to 10 number or a one-word waterfall, eyes-open grounding that works in any room, and a closing round that says when the next group meets. Keep each activity short and always allow a pass.

How do you run a check-in when cameras are off?

Move the check-in into chat. Ask for a number from 1 to 10, a one-word answer, or a choice between A, B and C, and give everyone the option to type pass. Read the answers back in general terms without naming who said what. Members who want to say more can unmute after the chat round.

What should I do if a group member is not alone during a video session?

Agree on a quiet signal in advance, such as typing an agreed word in chat, and don't ask the member to explain in front of the group. Let them listen with headphones, answer in chat or pass until their space is private again. Follow your program's policy on privacy and on confirming where members are joining from.

How do you end a virtual group therapy session?

Flag the end ten minutes early, run a short closing round in the same order as the check-in, and say and post the time of the next group. Then let everyone say goodbye at once and stay on as facilitator until the last member has left, so the session ends on purpose instead of with a sudden drop.

What should I do if a member disconnects during a telehealth group?

Set a standing rule in the first session: if you drop, rejoin with the same link. Tell the group briefly that the member lost connection and carry on. If the member drops during a hard moment, or doesn't return when you expected them to, follow your program's procedures rather than improvising a response in front of the group.
