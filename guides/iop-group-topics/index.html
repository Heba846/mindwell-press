IOP group topics: 78 session ideas for adult mental health IOP and PHP groups

By Heba El Garhy · Published October 25, 2026 · Updated November 9, 2026 · Editorial standards

Running groups five days a week uses up topics fast, and the usual lists online mix icebreaker games with substance-use worksheets. These are 78 group topics for adult mental health IOP and PHP programs, sorted by group type: process, skills, psychoeducation, and extra sessions for PHP. Each one stands on its own, so it works in a group where members join on different days.

How to choose today's topic

  1. Check who is in the room. If someone is on their first day, pick a topic that needs no earlier session, and plan a two-minute orientation before you start.
  2. Balance the day. Pair a teaching group, skills or psychoeducation, with a process group, so members both learn something and use it with each other.
  3. Follow the room, within limits. If the group keeps raising sleep or loneliness, move that topic up the rotation. Don't rebuild the day around one member's crisis; handle that through your program's procedure.
  4. Plan two depths. Have one reflection question for first-time members and one for people who have heard the skill before. Returning members disengage when every session is pitched at day one.

The codes next to each topic match the session codes in Volume 1 of the Open-Enrollment Program Series, where each one is a full session plan. You don't need the book to use this list.

Process group topics (18)

A process group gives members room to talk about what is happening for them now, with each other. The topic is a starting point, not a lesson plan. Each of these works on its own, so a first-day member can take part without having heard the last one.

  1. Arriving PR-01
    What it is like to be here today, for a first-day member and a returning one alike.
  2. Carrying the Week PR-02
    What members brought in with them, and what they can set down for the hour.
  3. The Mask and the Mirror PR-03
    The gap between how members come across and how they feel inside.
  4. Frustration and Stuckness PR-04
    Where progress feels slow, and what being stuck looks like day to day.
  5. Trust and Safety Here PR-05
    What makes it easier or harder to speak honestly in this room.
  6. The Inner Critic PR-06
    The judging voice, where it learned its lines, and how members answer it.
  7. Grief, Loss, and What Changed PR-07
    Losses of people, roles, health or plans, and what changed afterward.
  8. Hope and Doubt PR-08
    Holding both at once without having to settle which one wins.
  9. Connection and Isolation PR-09
    Pulling away, reaching out, and what gets in the way of each.
  10. Resilience and Quiet Wins PR-10
    Small things that went better, which members tend to skip past.
  11. Giving and Receiving Feedback PR-11
    Practicing honest, kind feedback with each other in real time.
  12. Anger and Boundaries PR-12
    What anger is pointing to, and where a limit may be needed.
  13. Shame and Self-Forgiveness PR-13
    What shame makes people hide, and what easing self-blame could look like.
  14. Identity Beyond the Diagnosis PR-14
    Who members are apart from symptoms, labels and treatment.
  15. Navigating Outside Relationships PR-15
    Family, partners, friends and work while someone is in treatment.
  16. Setbacks and Recovery Momentum PR-16

  18. Endings, Goodbyes, and Continuity PR-18
    Saying goodbye well, for the member who leaves and the group that stays.

Skills group topics (18)

Skills groups teach one concrete skill, practice it in the room and plan one use before the next group. The first three modules follow Linehan's DBT skill areas. The rest draw on cognitive behavioral therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy and behavioral activation.

Mindfulness

  1. Observe and Describe SK-MF-01
    Noticing experience and putting it into words without judging it.
  2. Grounding When Overwhelmed SK-MF-02
    Using the senses and the body to come back to the present moment.
  3. Attention Training SK-MF-03
    Practicing where attention goes, and bringing it back when it drifts.

Emotion regulation

  1. Emotions, Urges, and Actions SK-ER-01
    How a feeling, the urge it brings and the action that follows connect.
  2. Check the Facts and Opposite Action SK-ER-02
    Testing whether an emotion fits the facts, and acting opposite when it does not help.
  3. Reducing Emotional Vulnerability SK-ER-03
    Sleep, food, movement and routine as the base for steadier moods.

Distress tolerance

  1. Crisis Survival Skills SK-DT-01
    Getting through an intense moment without making the situation worse.
  2. Radical Acceptance and Turning the Mind SK-DT-02
    Accepting what cannot be changed right now, and choosing acceptance again.
  3. Self-Soothing with the Senses and a Distraction Plan SK-DT-03
    A personal list of what calms and what distracts, ready before it is needed.

Thoughts (CBT and ACT)

  1. The Thought-Feeling-Behavior Connection and Common Thinking Traps SK-CG-01
    The CBT triangle and the thinking patterns that keep low mood and anxiety going.
  2. Examining the Evidence and Thought Reframing SK-CG-02
    Weighing a thought against the facts and finding a more balanced version.
  3. Cognitive Defusion and Noticing Hooked Thoughts SK-CG-03
    Stepping back from a thought instead of arguing with it.

Behavioral activation

  1. Activity Scheduling and the Pleasure-Mastery Balance SK-BA-01
    Planning activities that bring enjoyment or a sense of achievement.
  2. Values-Based Action and Overcoming Avoidance SK-BA-02
    Choosing actions by what matters, even when avoidance pulls the other way.
  3. Graded Task Assignment and Habit Stacking SK-BA-03
    Breaking tasks into small steps and attaching new habits to existing ones.

Interpersonal effectiveness

  1. Objective Effectiveness and Assertive Communication SK-IE-01
    Asking clearly for what you need.
  2. Relationship Effectiveness and Boundary Setting SK-IE-02
    Holding a limit while keeping the relationship.
  3. Self-Respect Effectiveness and Saying No SK-IE-03
    Responding to others in a way that fits your own values.

How do I plan a week of IOP groups?

Plan the week around one skills module and fill the rest of each day around who is in the room. Run one module a week, three sessions, pair each teaching group with a process group, and let the census choose the psychoeducation topics. Because every session on this page stands alone, the week works whoever arrived on Monday.

The grids below are a worked example built from the lists above, not a standard and not a copy of any published calendar. Your program's schedule, payer and clinical team decide what a real week looks like.

Why a rotation instead of a fixed order?

A rotation works when members arrive and leave on different days; a fixed order does not. In a fixed order, a member who arrives at session 9 starts in the middle of a sequence and never hears sessions 1 to 8. In a rotation the cycle comes back around, so every member eventually meets every area.

That is why the two fixed points of the week are small. The skills module is fixed for the week, and each day pairs a teaching group with a process group. Everything else stays open until you have checked who is in the room. For how a rotation works session by session, see How to run a rolling-admission therapy group.

What does a five-day week look like?

A five-day week with two groups a day gives you five process groups, three skills sessions and two psychoeducation sessions. Here is one such week, in the first week of the skills cycle (mindfulness).

The skills module is the only fixed part of that grid. The two psychoeducation slots are the flexible ones: swap PE-05 or PE-13 for whatever the room needs this week, such as PE-02 if several members are struggling with anxiety. Process topics can repeat from week to week, because what a process group becomes depends on the people in it.

Keep PR-17 and PR-18 (transition and endings) out of the general rotation. Bring them in for weeks when someone is stepping down.

How long should a full rotation run?

Run the skills modules as a six-week cycle, one module a week. There are six skill areas on this page and three sessions in each, so a member who attends every day for six weeks meets all of them, and the cycle then starts again.

If a typical stay is shorter than six weeks, decide which modules you most want every member to meet and put those first, and be clear with the team about which ones a short stay will miss.

The Open-Enrollment Program Series also runs its rotation over six weeks. Which module opens the cycle does not matter, because no session depends on an earlier one.

How does the plan change for a PHP day?

A PHP day holds more groups, so keep the same teaching-plus-process spine and add PHP groups on top. Do not stretch the IOP grid across a longer day. The example below adds one PHP group to each day; a longer day adds more, drawn from the four PHP families above (daily routines, safety and relapse planning, transition, and practice in the room).

Run the safety and relapse planning groups only within your program's risk procedures. For how many hours a week each level of care involves, and how a PHP day differs, see PHP vs IOP group therapy.

How does the week change for three or four days?

On a shorter schedule, keep one skills module a week and shrink psychoeducation first. Because the module is the unit, the six-week cycle stays six weeks whether the program runs three days or five. Programs run different numbers of days, and the Open-Enrollment Program Series includes rotation calendars for 3-day IOP, 4-day extended IOP and 5-day PHP programs.

What do I do about low census, absences and holidays?

When a day is short, drop in a set order so the choice is made before the day, not during it. This is a practice suggestion; follow your program's policy where it has one.

  1. Drop first: extra PHP groups and psychoeducation. Both are chosen by the room, so nothing depends on them. Move the psychoeducation topic to the next day.
  2. Shorten next: the skills session. Keep one teaching point, one practice and one planned use before the next group.
  3. Keep last: the check-in, the process group and the orientation for a first-day member. They are what makes a small or short group still work as a group.

With only two or three members, run the skills session as a practice conversation rather than a presentation. The topic stays the same; what changes is that each member applies it out loud. Whether a shortened or small group meets your program's or payer's requirements for hours and documentation is a program question, so ask it before you design around it.

For absences, let the module slide rather than compress it. A member who misses a session picks it up the next time the cycle comes around, or through a catch-up page if they are stepping down (see how to handle a member stepping down mid-rotation). For a closure day, skip that day's slot and resume with the next session the following day, instead of squeezing two sessions into one. For the weeks around a winter closure, see holiday group therapy topics for IOP and PHP groups.

Who decides the plan, and how far ahead should it go?

Who decides varies by program. A common split is that the clinical lead or program director sets the spine and the facilitator chooses the day's topic within it. Plan the spine six weeks ahead, which costs nothing because it is a fixed cycle. Plan the detail of the week one week ahead at most, and confirm each day's psychoeducation and process topic after you have looked at who is in the room.

Planning further than that tends to be wasted, because census changes the week. To open any session, pick a prompt from 60 group therapy check-in questions.

Common questions

What is the difference between process, skills and psychoeducation groups?

A skills group teaches and practices one concrete skill. A psychoeducation group explains a condition or process, such as anxiety or sleep, in plain language. A process group gives members room to talk with each other about what is happening for them now, with the facilitator guiding the conversation rather than teaching.

How many groups does an IOP or PHP day usually include?

It varies by program and payer. IOP days are commonly about three hours, often two or three groups, while PHP days are longer and need more groups. That is why PHP programs need a larger topic list.

See PHP vs IOP group therapy for what else changes for the facilitator.

Can I use these topics in a rolling-admission group?

Yes. Every topic on this list is written to stand alone, so it works when new members join on any day. Give each session a short orientation for first-day members and separate reflection questions for first-time and returning members.

How do I plan a week of IOP groups?

Build the week around one skills module, three sessions, and pair every teaching group with a process group. Fill the remaining teaching slots with psychoeducation topics chosen by who is in the room. Because each session stands alone, the plan works when members join on different days. Repeat the six skill modules as a six-week rotation, and adjust it to your program's schedule.

How many groups a day should an IOP run?

There is no single right number. It follows from the length of your program's day and the session length it uses, so check your schedule and payer requirements. If you are planning from scratch, two groups a day is a workable start: one teaching group, skills or psychoeducation, and one process group. Longer days add a third group.

What do I drop first when an IOP group day runs short?

Drop psychoeducation and any extra PHP groups first, because the room chooses them and nothing depends on them. Shorten the skills session next: one teaching point, one practice, one planned use. Keep the check-in, the process group and the first-day orientation last. Whether a shortened day meets your program's or payer's requirements is a question for your program.
