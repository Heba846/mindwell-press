Holiday group therapy topics for adult IOP and PHP groups

By Heba El Garhy · Published October 26, 2026 · Editorial standards

It's the second week of December. A member mentions, almost in passing, that the program closes for four days and she has no idea what she'll do with them. Good holiday group therapy topics make room for that moment. They hold grief that gets louder this time of year, loneliness nobody else seems to name, family stress, and a schedule that suddenly has holes in it. This guide gives you eight session topics, openers that don't assume anyone celebrates or has family nearby, and a plan for the last group before a break.

The examples below come from an adult IOP group with rolling admission: seven members, a 50-minute session, three weeks before a winter closure. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional.

Why holidays are a high-risk window

From late November to early January, the culture around your members runs on one message: be happy, be with family, be grateful. Many adults in IOP and PHP care don't fit that picture, and the gap between the message and their real life can hurt.

Stress is common for almost everyone. In a 2023 poll from the American Psychological Association, 89% of US adults said concerns such as money, missing loved ones and expected family conflict caused them stress during the holidays. Thirty-eight percent named missing family or loved ones. SAMHSA notes that the season can bring stress, sadness and loneliness, and that it can be especially hard for people living with mental health and substance use conditions.

For members in a day program, a few things tend to pile up at once:

  • Loss of structure. The program may close or run a reduced schedule. For some members, group is the main thing that organizes the day.
  • Anniversaries. A death, a divorce, a hospitalization or a lost job may have happened at this time of year. Members may not connect a rough week to the date.
  • Family contact, or its absence. Some members will see relatives who are part of their stress. Others are estranged, grieving, far from home or simply alone.
  • Alcohol and substances. Gatherings, work parties and gifts often center on drinking. That matters for members in recovery and for members whose medications don't mix with alcohol.
  • Money pressure. Gifts, travel and time off work can strain a budget that was already tight.
  • Different traditions, or none. Your group may include people who celebrate Christmas, Hanukkah, Kwanzaa, the Lunar New Year later in winter, other holidays, or nothing at all. Some members find the whole season irrelevant. That's fine too.

None of this means every member is in crisis. It means the weeks around a break are a good time to plan on purpose, rather than run the usual rotation and hope.

8 holiday-safe session topics

Each topic below works as a standalone session, so a member admitted the week before closure can join without missing anything. Keep the language neutral: "the next few weeks" or "the days the program is closed," not "your holiday." If you need ideas for the rest of the year, see the full list of 78 IOP group topics.

1. Grief, loss and what changed

The season often brings an empty chair, a changed tradition or a first year without someone. Loss here can include people, health, a role, a home or a plan. Let members define their own loss, and don't require anyone to name it out loud.

"Sometimes this time of year shows us what's different now. You can share something that changed for you, or just listen today."

2. Loneliness versus being alone

Being alone and feeling lonely aren't the same thing. Some members will spend the break alone and feel fine. Others will be in a full house and feel invisible. Name both, then help each person pick one small, realistic point of contact for the closed days, like a phone call, a library visit or a support meeting.

3. The season on screen versus the week you'll actually have

Ads and social media show one version of the holidays. Ask members to describe the kind of week they actually expect, in practical terms. Then ask what would make that real week a little easier. This lowers the pressure to feel a certain way and moves the group toward planning.

4. Boundaries with family and other people

Some members will spend time with people who are hard to be around. Others have limited or no contact, by choice or not. Keep the topic about "people you'll see, or won't see" rather than family by default. Practice one short sentence a member could use to end a conversation, change the subject or leave early.

"If you'll be around someone who's hard for you, what's one sentence you could say to give yourself a break? Let's write a few together. Nobody has to share who it's about."

5. Money pressure without the details

Money is a real stressor, and also a private one. Never ask members to share amounts, debts or income in group. Instead, talk about the pressure itself: expectations to give gifts, travel or host. Ask what a low-cost or no-cost version of the season could look like, and what members can say when someone expects more than they can give.

6. Gatherings, alcohol and having a way out

For members in recovery, or anyone who wants to drink less, holiday events can be loaded. Help members plan before the event: what they'll hold in their hand, who they could text, how they'll get home, and what time they'll leave. Keep it practical. Coordinate with your program's substance use services if the group mixes different treatment needs.

7. Shaping the days without program

When the program closes, the day can lose its shape. Have each member sketch one closed day in three blocks: morning, afternoon and evening. Each block gets one small task, one thing that feels good, and one point of contact. A plan doesn't have to be impressive. It has to be doable on a low-energy day.

8. Your support map for the break

Who and what helps, and which resource fits which moment? Have members list people, places and services, then sort them by "a hard afternoon," "a hard night" and "an emergency." This topic leads straight into the last group before the break, described below.

Openers that don't assume family or celebration

The opening question sets the tone. "What are your holiday plans?" sounds friendly, but it assumes the member celebrates, has plans and has people to make them with. A member who has none of those learns quickly that they don't fit the room.

Swap the common openers for neutral ones:

  • Instead of "What are your holiday plans?" try "What's one thing on your schedule in the next two weeks?"
  • Instead of "Who are you spending the holidays with?" try "Who's one person, or one place, you'd like some contact with during the break?"
  • Instead of "What's your favorite tradition?" try "What's something, big or small, that helps you get through this time of year?"
  • Instead of "Are you excited for the break?" try "On a scale of 0 to 10, how ready do you feel for the days the program is closed?"

A few more openers that work in mixed groups:

  • "One word for how this time of year usually feels for you."
  • "Is the next month busier, quieter or about the same as usual for you?"
  • "What's one thing you'd like to keep doing while the program is closed?"
  • "What's one thing you'd like the group to know before the break? You can pass."

Always offer the pass. Model a short answer yourself so members know a long story isn't expected. For more prompts sorted by length and group type, see 60 group therapy check-in questions.

Planning around program closures

Closures are easier on members when they aren't a surprise. Start planning three to four weeks out:

  • Announce dates early and often. Say the closure dates in group, post them on the wall and give a printed copy. Repeat them for every new admission. A member who joined last Tuesday may not know.
  • Plan for low census. Attendance often drops in the weeks around a holiday. Choose standalone sessions that still work with two or three members, so a small group still feels like real treatment.
  • Flag members who may need more support. Bring concerns to your treatment team before the closure, not on the last day. Individual check-ins, step-down timing and outside referrals are team decisions. Follow your program's procedures.
  • Know your own coverage. Find out who is on call, what the after-hours line is, and what staff should do if a member calls during the break. Members will ask you.
  • Avoid big changes right before a break. If possible, don't start a heavy new topic in the final session. Opening something you can't close for two weeks rarely helps.

Fictional example. In our example group, the facilitator posts the closure dates three weeks ahead. A member named Rosa, admitted eight days before the closure, says in check-in that she didn't know the program closes. The facilitator gives her the printed schedule and makes a note to raise her start date with the team, so they can decide together how to plan her first two weeks.

A safety-minded last group before the break

The last group before a closure has one job: help each member leave with a plan they can use. Keep it calm, structured and short on new material. A simple outline for a 50-minute group:

  1. Check-in (8 minutes). A short, neutral prompt, such as a 0 to 10 readiness rating for the break.
  2. Name what's coming (5 minutes). Say the closure dates and the date group restarts. Normalize mixed feelings about the break, including relief.
  3. Build the support card (20 minutes). Each member fills in a small card or half sheet: two people or places for a hard afternoon, one plan for the day they expect to be hardest, and the numbers below.
  4. Practice one step (10 minutes). Pick one item from the card and rehearse it. For example, what a member would actually say when calling a friend or a support line.
  5. Close (7 minutes). One sentence from each member: "One thing I'll do on the first closed day." Then remind the group when you'll meet again.

Every support card should include:

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988 (US), any time.
  • Program after-hours number: [add your program's after-hours number here].
  • Emergency services: 911 for an emergency.

Write the numbers on the card in group, rather than handing out a sheet members may never read. If anything a member says during this session raises a safety concern, stop the planned activity and follow your program's procedures. This guide doesn't replace your program's risk assessment or crisis protocol.

Plan the first group after the break too. Open with a gentle check-in about how the closed days went, and expect that some members will return in a different place than they left. A short reconnection round helps the group find its footing again.
