Co-facilitating group therapy: roles, signals and a 5-minute debrief

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 20, 2026 · Editorial standards

Co-facilitating group therapy works best when two facilitators agree before group who leads each part, who watches the room, which quiet signals mean "pause" or "take over," and who steps out if a member leaves. They then spend five minutes after group comparing what each saw. Most problems come from what was never agreed.

The examples below come from an imagined adult IOP group with rolling admission: nine members, a 50-minute skills session, one member on their first day, and two facilitators named Dana and Luis. Every member, facilitator and line of dialogue is fictional. Programs vary, so follow your program's policy on staffing, coverage and safety.

Why co-facilitation needs a plan

A second facilitator only helps if both of you know what the other is doing. The American Group Psychotherapy Association's practice guidelines name the benefits of co-leading: modeling healthy interpersonal behavior, a second set of observations about the group, and feeling more supported, which reduces therapist anxiety.

The same guidelines call the core challenge how differences between the two therapists will be negotiated. They list agreeing on how to share leadership roles and tasks as part of the commitment co-leaders make. In other words, the benefits are not automatic. They come from preparation.

Federal training material on group therapy from SAMHSA, based on its Treatment Improvement Protocol 41, describes cotherapy as an effective way to blend diverse skills, resources and therapeutic perspectives. It adds that a cotherapy pair gives members an opportunity to watch adaptive behavior. Members learn from how the two of you treat each other.

In an open-enrollment group, this matters more. New members watch the facilitators to learn how the room works. Two people who give mixed messages about the rules teach a new member that the rules are negotiable. For more on how new members read a group, see how to run a rolling-admission therapy group.

How should co-facilitators divide lead and support roles?

Pick one lead and one support for each segment of the session, and say it out loud before group. The lead talks and moves the plan forward. The support watches the room, tracks time and catches what the lead can't see.

You can swap roles between segments. Many pairs have one person lead check-in and the other lead the skill. What matters is that, at any moment, both of you know who holds the floor.

Sit where you can see each other. Across the circle from each other works for most rooms. Sitting side by side makes signals hard to catch and can look like a panel facing the members.

Decide in advance how the support adds to the lead. A simple rule is that the support speaks when invited ("Luis, anything to add?") or after a signal, and keeps it to one or two sentences. Two people teaching at once is harder to follow than one.

If you are a trainee co-leading with a senior clinician, agree on what you will lead. Supporting for a whole session can be useful practice, but say so out loud so the members don't wonder why one of you never speaks.

Agreeing on signals before group

Agree on three or four quiet signals before the session starts, and keep them small enough that members don't notice. A signal is a way to say something without stopping the room.

Most pairs need a signal for each of these:

  • "Time." The support gives a time cue for the segment. A glance at the clock, then at the lead, is often enough.
  • "Look over there." The support has noticed a member who seems upset, withdrawn or about to leave. A slight nod toward that side of the room works.
  • "Can I come in?" The support has something useful to add. A raised finger or leaning forward lets the lead decide when.
  • "Please take over." The lead is stuck, tired or too close to the topic. Agree on one clear cue, like closing your notebook.

Spend two minutes before group on the plan, not only the signals. Say who leads each segment, what you know about today's members, and what each of you will do if a member gets very upset or leaves the room. The AGPA guidelines include not assuming your co-leader knows your plans and ideas, but communicating them clearly, as part of the co-leader commitment.

Here is a fictional pre-group exchange that takes about two minutes:

Dana: "I'll lead check-in and the close. You take the skill. There's a new member, Priya, today."
Luis: "Got it. I'll sit near the door side. If someone leaves, I'll follow and you keep the group. Clock glance means five minutes left."
Dana: "Notebook closed means take over. Okay?"
Luis: "Okay."

If you co-lead often with the same person, your signals will settle. With a new partner, or a float staff member covering, go over them every time. Assuming the other person knows is where most mix-ups start.

Who steps out when a member leaves the room?

Decide before group which facilitator follows a member who leaves and which stays, and stick to it. Usually the support steps out and the lead stays, so the session keeps its shape. The group should never be left without a facilitator.

When a member walks out, the room watches what you do next. A calm, short line from the lead helps the others settle:

"Luis is going to check in with Sam. We'll keep going with the skill. Sam is welcome back anytime."

Keep it to that. Don't guess aloud why the member left, and don't ask the group to explain it. Members may feel worried, guilty or curious. If the room stays unsettled, a short grounding step or a pause for one breath can help before you go on.

The facilitator who steps out has two jobs: find out whether the member is safe and what they need, and decide with the member whether returning is right today. If anything you see or hear raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures and request support through your program's route. A facilitator's check-in in the hallway is not a risk assessment.

Agree on what happens when the member comes back. A quiet nod and an offer of the right to pass is often enough. Avoid recapping what they missed in front of the group. If you are leading alone and a member leaves, the rules change: don't leave the group unattended to follow them; use your program's way of getting help.

What if you disagree in front of the group?

Small differences in front of the group are fine and can even be useful. Members see two adults handle a difference calmly. Big disagreements about a rule, a safety decision or the plan belong after group, not in the room.

The AGPA guidelines say that differences between co-leaders need to be contained and explored, and that this work needs to occur before the start of the group, during the group sessions, and in debriefings after the group sessions. Containing a difference in the moment is a skill you can plan for.

Three common situations and what to do:

  • You'd explain the skill differently. Let the lead finish. If your version would help, signal and add one sentence: "Another way to think about it is..." Credit the lead's version first.
  • You gave members different directions. Fix it quickly and together. "Dana and I said two different things. Let's go with Dana's: one sentence each." A short, shared correction keeps the rule clear for the new member.
  • A member asks one of you to overrule the other. Don't take the bait. "We'll talk about that as a team after group. For today, the plan stays the same." Then talk about it after.

What not to do: correct your co-facilitator's facts in front of members, roll your eyes or sigh, or side with a member against your partner. Members in IOP and PHP often watch closely for conflict between staff. The goal is not to look perfect. It is to show that a difference can be handled without anyone being shamed.

If a member is taking up the space between you, for example by talking over one facilitator but not the other, see how to handle a group member who monopolizes the session for redirect lines either of you can use.

The 5-minute debrief

Spend five minutes together right after group, before either of you starts notes. Compare what each of you saw, agree on anything that needs follow-up, and name one thing to change next time. Five minutes is enough if you keep to a set order.

  1. Safety and follow-up (1 minute). Did anything need a report, a hand-off or a check-in with the treatment team? Agree on who does it, today.
  2. What each saw (2 minutes). The support goes first, because they watched the room. "Priya went quiet after the second example." "Sam left at 10:20 and came back at 10:31."
  3. How the pairing worked (1 minute). Did the signals work? Did anyone talk over the other? Keep it about the process, not about who was right.
  4. One change (1 minute). Agree on one thing to do differently next session, and write it where you'll both see it.

Agree on who writes which part of the notes. Each facilitator usually documents the segments they led, using the same facts from the debrief so the notes don't contradict each other. For note wording, see group therapy progress notes for IOP and PHP.

Some days five minutes won't be enough, such as after a hard session or a real disagreement between you. Book a longer talk or bring it to supervision. The AGPA guidelines describe successful co-leaders as able to work through conflicts and willing to receive supervision to explore their own role in the co-leadership.

Common questions

How do two facilitators split roles in a therapy group?

Pick one lead and one support for each segment, and say it before group starts. The lead talks and moves the plan. The support watches the room, tracks time, greets late arrivals and notices who leaves. You can swap roles between segments, such as one leading check-in and the other leading the skill. Both should always know who holds the floor.

What signals should co-facilitators agree on before group?

Agree on three or four small signals members won't notice: one for time, one for "look at that member," one for "can I add something," and one for "please take over." Go over them before each session, especially with a new partner or a float staff member. Also agree who will step out if a member leaves the room.

Which co-facilitator should leave the room when a member walks out?

Decide before group, and stick to it. Usually the support facilitator follows the member and the lead stays, so the session keeps its shape and the group is never left unattended. The lead gives the room one calm line and keeps going. If anything raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures. If you lead alone, don't leave the group to follow.

What should a co-facilitator debrief after group cover?

Keep it to five minutes and a set order. First, any safety issue or follow-up and who handles it. Second, what each facilitator saw, with the support going first. Third, how the pairing worked, such as missed signals. Fourth, one change for next time. Agree on who writes which part of the notes so they don't contradict each other.

Is it okay for co-facilitators to disagree in front of the group?

Small differences are fine and can model calm disagreement. Let the lead finish, then add one sentence that credits their version first. If you gave members different directions, fix it together quickly. Keep big disagreements about rules, safety or the plan for after group, and never correct, dismiss or side against your co-facilitator in front of members.
