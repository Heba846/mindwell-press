How to orient a new member to an open therapy group

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 2, 2026 · Editorial standards

Someone walks into an open group on their first day, and everyone else has been there before. The three minutes at the start of the session help, but they are not an orientation. This guide covers a short one you can give before or at a member's first group, with a script for the welcome and what to leave out.

Why the Newcomer Bridge is not enough

The Newcomer Bridge is a three-minute scripted opening that names the session's one idea and re-grounds everyone. It helps a first-day member follow the session, but it does not replace a proper orientation. Each new member should get a short individual or small-group orientation covering program structure, group norms and crisis resources before or at their first group, not squeezed into the first three minutes of a session.

What to cover before the first group

Five minutes is enough if you stay to five points. Adapt each to your program's policies.

  1. Structure. How the day runs, how many groups there are, and where breaks and sign-in happen.
  2. How the group works. That people join on any day, that each session stands on its own, and that they are not expected to have heard earlier sessions.
  3. Norms. One speaker at a time, respect for what people share, and your program's confidentiality policy, including its limits.
  4. The right to pass. Any question or activity can be passed. They do not have to disclose anything to take part.
  5. If it gets to be too much. Who to tell, whether they can step out and come back, and the crisis resources your program uses. In the US, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline can be reached by call or text.

A welcome script

"Welcome. This is a group where people join on different days, so there's no catching up to do. Each session stands on its own. You'll hear the same ground rules every time: one speaker at a time, what's said here stays here as far as our program allows, and you can pass on anything. Today we're working on [skill]. If something gets to be too much, tell me or step out for a moment, and you're welcome to come back."

Follow your program's policy on introductions. Some programs use first names only. Let members decide how much to say about themselves.

What not to ask a first-day member

  • Do not ask them to share their story. A new member does not owe the group a history.
  • Do not call on them first. Open a question to the room and let returning members go first.
  • Do not make them the example. Invite returning members to demonstrate, never assign a newcomer.
  • Do not assume they know the vocabulary. Define a skill's name the first time you use it, in one sentence.

When the new member only watches

Many people spend a first group watching, and that is a legitimate way to take part. Offer a smaller task, such as one word for how they are arriving, and accept a pass. A first group where someone attended, followed the activity and declined to speak is a first group that went as expected.

Documenting a first group

Record what you delivered and what you observed: that orientation was given, what the member did in the session and what you offered. Do not infer feelings or engagement you did not observe, and keep other members out of the note. Common questions

How do you orient a new member to an open therapy group?

Give a short orientation before or at their first group, covering program structure, how the open group works, group norms, the right to pass, and who to tell if things get to be too much, including crisis resources. A three-minute session opening helps, but it does not replace it.

What should I say to a new member joining an ongoing group?

Welcome them, explain that people join on different days and each session stands on its own, repeat the ground rules and say they can pass on anything. Tell them today's skill in a sentence and who to tell if something becomes too much. Follow your program's policy on introductions.

Should a new group member be asked to introduce themselves or share their story?

No. A first-day member does not owe the group a history. Open questions to the room, let returning members go first and offer a small task, such as one word for how they are arriving. Accept a pass, and let members decide how much to say about themselves.

What if a new member only watches in their first group?

That is a legitimate way to take part. Offer a smaller task, accept a pass and record what you observed: that the member attended, followed the activity and declined to speak when invited. Do not infer engagement or feelings you did not observe, and keep other members out of the note.
