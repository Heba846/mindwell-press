Group therapy ground rules for adult IOP and PHP groups

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 3, 2026 · Updated November 13, 2026 · Editorial standards

Group therapy ground rules for adult IOP and PHP groups should fit on one page: confidentiality and its limits, the right to pass, one speaker at a time, shared airtime, speaking for yourself, no put-downs, phones away, and stepping out safely. In an open group, repeat them in about 60 seconds whenever someone new joins.

The examples below come from an imagined adult IOP group with rolling admission. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional. Rules, policies and consequences vary by program, so treat this wording as a starting point and follow your program's policy where it differs.

Why do ground rules matter more when members change weekly?

In an open group, the rules are never settled. A closed group can agree on its rules in week one and refer back to them. A rolling-admission group has someone in the room who wasn't there when the rules were last said.

That changes three things for the facilitator.

  • New members learn the rules by watching. If the first check-in they see runs long and nobody says anything, that becomes the rule. What you allow on a new member's first day teaches as much as what you say.
  • Returning members drift. A member on day twelve has heard the rules so often that they stop registering. Short, regular repeats keep them in view without a lecture.
  • The agreement has to be remade. The group that agreed to keep things private on Monday is not the same group on Wednesday. Each new person has to hear the agreement and have the chance to say yes to it.

There is an ethics reason too. The American Counseling Association's Code of Ethics (standard B.4.a) says counselors in group work "clearly explain the importance and parameters of confidentiality for the specific group." In an open group, that explanation comes up again and again. Build it into the routine so it doesn't depend on your memory on a busy day.

What are the 8 core group therapy ground rules?

These eight cover most of what goes wrong in an adult group, and each one can be read aloud in a single breath. The quoted wording is written for members. The note after it is for you.

  1. "What you hear here stays here." Other members' names and stories don't leave the room, in conversation, in texts or on social media. You are always free to talk about your own experience. Pair this rule with its limits. See the next section.
  2. "You can pass." You can pass on any question or activity, with no reason needed. Make this real by passing without comment when someone uses it. A pass followed by "Are you sure?" isn't a pass.
  3. "One person talks at a time." Side conversations make it hard to hear and easy to feel talked about. This is the rule you will use most, and it's the easiest one to enforce kindly.
  4. "We share the airtime." Everyone gets a turn, so the facilitator may pause you to make room. Saying this ahead of time makes it easier to redirect later. For the moment itself, see how to handle a group member who monopolizes the session.
  5. "Speak for yourself, and ask before giving advice." Say "I found" instead of "you should." Advice from a peer can land as pressure, even when it's offered with care. Teach members to ask first: "Would it help to hear what worked for me?"
  6. "No put-downs." You can disagree with an idea. You can't insult, mock or threaten a person. Name this one plainly, because members who have had bad group experiences are listening for it.
  7. "Phones silent and out of sight." Keep this one practical. If a member is expecting an urgent call about a child or a job, have them tell you before group so an exception doesn't look like a broken rule. Always include a no-photos, no-recording line.
  8. "If it gets to be too much, tell us, or step out and come back." Members need to know who to tell and whether staff will check on them. Fill in those details from your own program's procedures, and say them aloud.

Add your program's rules. Attendance, lateness, coming to group under the influence, and contact between members outside of group are usually program policy. Don't improvise these in the room. State them the way your program wrote them, and put them on the printable in the blank space provided.

How to explain the limits of confidentiality

Be clear about two different things: what members agree to, and what staff may have to do. The NASW Code of Ethics (standard 1.07) asks social workers in group work to seek agreement on keeping others' information private, including whether it may be shared electronically or outside sessions. It also says to tell participants that social workers "cannot guarantee that all participants will honor such agreements."

Say it directly. Most adults respect the honesty.

"We all agree to keep each other's stories in this room, and I can't promise that everyone always will. So share what feels okay to share. Staff also have limits on what we can keep private. Those were in your paperwork, and I'm happy to go over them with you."

Your program sets the actual limits. Use its consent language rather than writing your own, and check that what you say in the room matches what members signed.

What ground rules should you add for telehealth groups?

Video groups need rules about where members join from, because you can't see who else is in the room. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services' best-practice guide on group teletherapy advises making clear what is and isn't acceptable. Its examples of common rules include having cameras on, attending from a room where the member can be alone, and using the raise-hand feature to speak. It also suggests first names only during introductions.

These add-ons cover the same ground in member wording:

  • "Join from a private spot." If you can't be alone, use headphones and tell the facilitator privately in chat.
  • "First names only on your screen." Check your display name before you join.
  • "No recordings or screenshots." That covers any device, not just the video app.
  • "Stay put for the session." Don't join while driving or from a public place.
  • "Use the raise-hand button or chat to get a turn." It solves most of the talking-over problem on video.
  • "Follow our camera rule." Say what your program requires and what to do if a member needs a camera break.

Your program may also require members to confirm where they are at the start of each session. If it does, make that part of the routine, and do it the way your procedures describe. For check-ins, grounding and closings built for video, see telehealth group therapy activities for virtual IOP.

How do you review the rules in 60 seconds when someone new joins?

Say the rules to the whole room, keep it short, and let returning members help. A long recitation tells a new member that the rules are a formality. A quick one tells them this is how the group always starts.

A sample script, about 60 seconds when read aloud:

"Before we start, the quick version of our rules, since Dana's joining us today. What you hear here stays here. You can tell people about your own experience, just not anyone else's. I can't promise everyone will always keep to that, and staff have some limits too, which are in your paperwork. You can pass on anything, no reason needed. One person talks at a time, and we share the airtime, so I may pause you to make room. Speak for yourself, and ask before giving advice. No put-downs. Phones silent and put away. And if it gets to be too much, tell me or step out, and you're welcome to come back. Marcus, anything you'd add from your time here?"

Three small choices make this work:

  • Ask a returning member to add one thing. It keeps them awake and shows the new member that the rules belong to the group. Let them pass too.
  • Don't ask the new member to agree out loud. Saying "Does that work for you, Dana?" in front of everyone can feel like a test. Check in privately instead.
  • Don't make the new member the reason for the delay. Mention that they're joining, then move on. They already feel watched.

This review doesn't replace a proper orientation. For what to cover before a first group, see how to orient a new member to an open therapy group. For where it fits in a session, see how to run a rolling-admission therapy group.

What do you do when a rule is broken?

Name the rule, not the person, then keep the group moving. Most broken rules need one calm sentence and nothing more. Save longer conversations for after group, and leave consequences to your program.

A phone ringsSay: "Quick reminder for all of us, phones silent and out of sight." Next: Keep going. Talk privately only if it keeps happening.
A side conversation startsSay: "One at a time, so I can hear you both." Next: Offer the pair a turn, or let it go if it stops.
Unasked adviceSay: "Thanks for wanting to help. Priya, would advice be useful right now, or would you rather just be heard?" Next: Follow Priya's answer.
A member repeats another member's storySay: "Let's pause there. We keep each other's stories in the room." Next: Talk privately after group, and tell your supervisor if it happened outside of group.
An insult or mocking commentSay: "I'm stopping this. You can disagree, but we don't put each other down here." Next: Check on the member it was aimed at. Talk with the speaker privately.
A threat or a safety concernStop using the rules as your tool. Next: Follow your program's procedures.

After the moment, a few habits keep this from becoming a power struggle.

  • Assume they forgot. In an open group, the member may have missed the last review. Treat a first slip as a reminder, not a violation.
  • Hold the rule the same way for everyone. If returning members get a pass that new members don't, the group will notice quickly.
  • Talk after group when it's a pattern. Ask with curiosity what's getting in the way. A member who keeps checking a phone may be waiting on something that needs a plan.
  • Take repeated problems to the team. Removing a member or changing their schedule is a treatment-team decision. It isn't something to threaten in the room.

Then document what you saw and what you did, without labels. Example excerpt (fictional, not a complete note):

"During check-in, client repeated details of another member's disclosure from a prior session. Facilitator paused the discussion and restated the group's confidentiality agreement. Facilitator met with client after group to review the agreement. Client stated understanding. Supervisor informed per program policy."

Keep other members' identities out of the note. More examples are in group therapy progress notes in GIRP and DAP.

Common questions

What ground rules should a therapy group have?

Most adult IOP and PHP groups can start with eight: keep what you hear in the room, the right to pass, one speaker at a time, shared airtime, speaking for yourself, no put-downs, phones away, and stepping out if needed. Add your program's own policies on attendance, lateness, substances and outside contact, and explain the limits of confidentiality.

How do I explain confidentiality in a group therapy session?

Say what you are asking of members, then say what you cannot promise. Members agree to keep other people's stories and names in the room. Staff explain the limits under your program's policy. The NASW Code of Ethics says participants should be told social workers cannot guarantee that every member will honor the agreement, so say that plainly.

How often should I go over group rules in an open group?

Review them every time someone new joins, and keep it to about 60 seconds. In a rolling-admission program that can mean several times a week. Say the rules to the whole room, not at the new member, and ask returning members to help by naming one. A short, predictable review also reminds everyone what the group agreed to.

What do I do when a member breaks a group rule?

Name the rule, not the person, then move on. For example, say you want one speaker at a time so you can hear everyone. Hold longer conversations privately after group. Bring repeated problems to your supervisor or treatment team, because consequences are a program decision. If what happened raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures.
