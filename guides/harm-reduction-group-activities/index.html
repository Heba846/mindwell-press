Harm reduction group activities for AOD day programs

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 12, 2026 · Editorial standards

A harm reduction group helps members lower the harms of alcohol and other drug use whatever their goal is this week: stopping, cutting down, or not ready yet. In a day program where someone new joins every week, each session has to stand alone. Below: eight stand-alone sessions, one complete sample session, and the safety lines that are not optional.

What is a harm reduction group?

A harm reduction group works on lowering the health, social and money harms of alcohol and other drug (AOD) use, and accepts that members arrive with different goals. Abstinence is one of those goals, not the entry ticket.

This is national policy in some countries, not a fringe position. Australia's National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 is built on harm minimisation, with three pillars: demand reduction, supply reduction and harm reduction. Canada's federal Canadian Drugs and Substances Strategy lists harm reduction alongside treatment and recovery in its substance use services.

Group programmes already work this way. In Ireland, the SAOL Project's twelve-module Reduce the Use programme asks each participant to choose their own goal: no substantial change for now, cutting down, or stopping.

Can members with different goals share one group?

Yes, as long as goal choice is part of the group's culture rather than a private exception. The facilitator's job is to keep every activity about the member's own pattern, so there is no single right answer for the room to drift toward.

Three habits make it work. Ask everyone to name their goal at check-in, in one line. Give reducing and stopping the same airtime and the same respect. And when a member says "not this week", treat it as information, not as a failure to argue with.

The risk to watch is the room quietly ranking goals, with the member who is stopping cast as the success story. If it happens, name it lightly: "In this group, every goal on the board gets the same attention."

How do you run it when someone joins every week?

Make every session complete in itself. A member on their first day should be able to take part without having heard last week's session, and a member in their sixth week should still find something new to work on.

Open with a three-minute bridge: the session's one idea in a sentence, one recall question for returning members, and the confidentiality rule. Our guide to running a rolling-admission group explains this Newcomer Bridge in full.

Then run one activity, ask two levels of question (one for first-timers, one for returning members) and close with one small step for the coming week. Keep the topics in a rotation that repeats, so anyone can join on any turn.

Eight stand-alone harm reduction sessions

Each session below has one idea and one activity, and none depends on another. Run them in any order as a rotation.

SessionThe one ideaActivity
1. Choosing my goal this weekYour goal is yours, and it can change.Goal card: stop, cut down, or not yet, plus one reason in your own words. (Full session below.)
2. A week in my usePatterns are easier to change once you can see them.A one-week snapshot: when, where, with whom, and what the use was doing for you.
3. High-risk momentsMost use has a trigger you can name.Map three people, places or feelings that make use more likely, and one way round each.
4. Riding an urgeAn urge rises and falls if you wait it out.Rate an urge from 0 to 10, wait five minutes with a chosen distraction, rate it again.
5. What it costs, what it givesUse does something for you, and it costs something too.A two-column honest ledger, with no judgement from the room about either column.
6. Saying no without a speechA short no is easier to repeat.Write and practise one refusal line in pairs, then try it with a harder push.
7. A lapse is informationA slip shows you what to plan for next time.Walk one recent slip through before, during and after, and pick one change.
8. Staying saferSome harms can be reduced even on days you use.Go through your service's own overdose and safety information. In Australia, show members where to get free naloxone.

A complete sample session: choosing my goal this week

This is a 60-minute session for an adult AOD day program or rehab group. It works for a first-day member and a member in their sixth week in the same room. The facilitator and members below are fictional.

Bridge (3 minutes)

Facilitator: "Today's one idea: your goal is yours, and it can change. If you're new, you don't need to know anything from before today. If you've been here a while, think about how your goal has moved since you started. What's said here stays here."

Check-in (10 minutes)

One line each: "This week my goal is to stop, cut down, or not decide yet." Members may pass. Write the three options on the board with no names next to them.

Activity: the goal card (20 minutes)

Each member fills in a card with four lines: my goal this week; one reason it matters to me; one thing that could get in the way; one person or place that helps.

In a fictional group, Dev, on his first day, writes "not decide yet" and "my sleep" as his reason. Lou, in her sixth week, crosses out "stop" and writes "cut down on weekdays", because stopping completely had not held. The facilitator treats both cards the same way: "What made you write that?"

Two levels of question (15 minutes)

For first-time members: "What's one thing you noticed about your use this week, without changing it?" For returning members: "How is the goal on your card different from the one you came in with, and what changed it?"

Close (12 minutes)

Each member names one small step for the coming week that fits their own goal. Then the safety line: "If your goal involves cutting down or stopping alcohol or tablets like benzodiazepines, talk to the team about a medical check first. That's a safety step, not a judgement."

Which safety lines are not optional?

Two topics need a fixed, sourced response in every harm reduction group: withdrawal and overdose. Neither is the group's to improvise.

Withdrawal. Healthdirect Australia, the Australian Government's health information service, warns that withdrawal symptoms from alcohol or benzodiazepines can be life-threatening, and advises talking to a doctor or an AOD service before stopping. When a member plans to stop or cut down either one, link them with medical review and follow your service's withdrawal policy.

Overdose. Australia's Take Home Naloxone program has offered naloxone free and without a prescription since 1 July 2022, through participating pharmacies and services such as AOD treatment centres. Outside Australia, use your own service's overdose response information.

Emergencies. If anything in group suggests immediate risk, pause the activity and follow your service's emergency procedure. Facilitators do not give dosing advice, and none of the activities above replaces clinical assessment.

What do programs call this in each country?

The work is similar across countries, but the wording on job ads and programme pages differs. Using the local term helps members and colleagues recognise the group.

CountryCommon service wording
AustraliaAOD (alcohol and other drugs) day program, residential rehab, harm minimisation
CanadaSubstance use services, concurrent disorders program
Ireland and UKAddiction services, drug and alcohol day programme
United StatesSubstance use disorder treatment, IOP and PHP

Common questions

What is a harm reduction group in addiction treatment?

A harm reduction group welcomes members whatever their goal is this week: stopping, cutting down, or not ready to change yet. Sessions focus on reducing the health, social and money harms of alcohol and other drug use rather than on abstinence alone. Australia's National Drug Strategy builds its approach on harm minimisation, and Canada's federal strategy lists harm reduction among its substance use services.

Can one group include members who want to stop and members who want to cut down?

Yes, if the facilitator makes goal choice part of the group's culture. Ask every member to name their own goal at check-in, treat reducing and stopping as equally worth discussing, and keep activities about the member's own pattern rather than one right answer. Ireland's Reduce the Use programme lets participants choose between no change, cutting down and stopping.

How do I run a harm reduction group when new members join every week?

Make every session stand alone. Open with a three-minute bridge that names the session's one idea and the confidentiality rule, run one activity that works on a member's first day, and close with one small step for the coming week. Keep a rotation of stand-alone topics, such as goal choice, high-risk moments and urges, so anyone can join on any day.

Is it safe for a group member to cut down alcohol on their own?

Not always. Withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines can be life-threatening, according to Healthdirect Australia, which advises talking to a doctor or an alcohol and other drug service before stopping. In a group, treat any plan to cut down or stop these substances as a cue to link the member with medical review, and follow your service's withdrawal policy.
