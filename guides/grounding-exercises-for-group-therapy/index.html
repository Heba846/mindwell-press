Grounding exercises for group therapy: 20 eyes-open ideas

By Heba El Garhy · Published October 30, 2026 · Editorial standards

One member is staring at the floor. Another is gripping the arms of the chair. A third looks half asleep from a new medication. Grounding exercises for group therapy have to work for all three at once, in a room where nobody chose to be together. This guide gives you 20 eyes-open, no-touch ideas, why each design choice matters, and how to pick one based on how the room feels today.

The examples below come from an adult PHP group with rolling admission: nine members, a 60-minute session, one member on their first day. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional.

What makes grounding safe in a group

Grounding is a set of simple ways to bring attention back to the present moment. In individual therapy you can tailor it to one person. In a group you can't. You have members with different histories, different medications and different reasons for being there. So the safest grounding exercises share three features.

  • Eyes open. Closing your eyes in a room of near strangers can feel exposed. For some members it makes them feel more distant, not less. Invite eyes to stay open, or to rest on a spot on the floor. If someone wants to close them, that's their choice, not your instruction.
  • No touch. No touching other members, no passing objects hand to hand, and no facilitator touch. Members can touch their own chair or the floor if they want to, but every exercise below also works without it.
  • Choice. Every exercise has a real right to pass. Say it out loud before you start, and mean it. "You can do this with us, do your own version, or just sit. All of those count."

Three more habits help. Keep it short: two to four minutes is plenty. Use plain, concrete instructions instead of soft, slow, hypnotic phrasing. And tell people what will happen before it happens, including when it ends. Predictability is part of what makes a group feel safe.

A note on framing. These are grounding exercises used as session openers or resets. Present them that way, not as a treatment in their own right. If a member becomes very distressed, stops responding, or can't come back to the room after an exercise, stop the exercise and follow your program's procedures.

Here is how one fictional facilitator, Dana, introduces grounding on a day with a new member:

"Before we start, we'll do a two-minute exercise to land in the room. Eyes stay open. You won't be asked to share anything personal. If you'd rather sit it out, that's fine. I'll tell you when it's over."

Outward-focus exercises: room, sound, objects

Outward-focus exercises point attention at the room instead of the body. They are the safest place to start, especially with new members or a group you don't know well yet.

  1. Colors in the room. "Silently find three things that are blue. Now three that are green." Members only answer in their heads. Optional: one person names one color they found.
  2. Count the corners. "Count how many corners you can see from your seat, including on furniture and windows." It's oddly absorbing and needs no answer.
  3. Describe it like a detective. Pick a neutral object in the room, such as the clock or a plant. Ask members to notice its shape, color and any marks on it. Then ask for one detail each, with passing allowed.
  4. Sounds near and far. "Notice one sound inside this room. Now one sound outside the room. Now one sound farther away." Eyes stay open. If the room is silent, the hum of the lights counts.
  5. Textures you can see. "Without touching anything, find something that looks rough, something smooth and something soft." This keeps the sensory focus without asking anyone to touch.
  6. Room alphabet. "Find something in the room that starts with A, then B, then C. Go as far as you like." Members work silently and stop whenever they want.
  7. Name the date and place. Ask the group to say together, or think, today's day, date, the name of the program and the city. It's simple orientation, and it works well on a heavy morning.
  8. Window or wall view. "Pick one spot on the wall or out the window. Notice everything about it for 30 seconds. I'll tell you when time is up."

These are also good on days when members aren't talking much. If silence is the bigger problem, pair one of these with the ideas in what to do when nobody talks in group therapy.

Gentle body exercises with a non-body option

Some members find body-focused grounding very helpful. Others find that attention on the body brings up pain, panic or memories. So every body exercise below comes with a non-body option, offered at the same time and in the same tone.

  1. Feet on the floor. "If you like, press your feet into the floor and notice the floor pushing back. Or look at your shoes and notice their color and laces." Both are equal options.
  2. Notice the chair. "Notice where the chair holds your weight. Or look at the chair next to you and notice its shape." No one has to describe what they notice.
  3. Hands on a surface. "Rest your hands on your legs or the table and notice the temperature. Or look at your hands and count the knuckles you can see."
  4. Small stretch. "If it's comfortable, roll your shoulders back once or twice. Or just look up at the ceiling and back down." Keep any movement small and seated, and never demonstrate anything that could hurt.
  5. Temperature check. "Is the room warm, cool or about right for you? Notice the air on your face, or just look at the thermostat or window and guess the temperature."
  6. Posture shift. "Change how you're sitting in any small way, so you're a little more comfortable. Or keep sitting exactly as you are. Both are fine."

Watch your language. "If you like" and "or" do a lot of work here. Avoid lines that tell members what they will feel, such as "You'll feel calm and heavy." Some won't, and they may decide they're doing it wrong.

Cognitive games that aren't tests

Thinking tasks can pull attention away from distress and into the present. The risk is that they feel like a test. A member who is sedated, anxious or struggling with concentration may freeze if a game feels like it has a right answer. Keep these private by default, with no score and no one put on the spot.

  1. Categories in your head. "Silently name as many kinds of fruit as you can in 30 seconds." Switch to dog breeds, cities or colors. Nobody reports a number.
  2. Count back by ones. "Count backward from 30 by ones, at your own pace." Counting by ones keeps it easy. Avoid serial sevens; that can feel like a mental status exam.
  3. Describe a routine task. "In your head, list the steps of making a cup of tea or coffee, from start to finish." It's familiar, ordinary and present-focused.
  4. Group word chain. Members say one word each that starts with the last letter of the previous word. Anyone can pass, and you jump in if someone stalls. Keep it playful.
  5. Song or TV theme recall. "Think of the name of a song you know. Now the name of a TV show." Nobody shares unless they want to. Keep it neutral; avoid asking for a song that "means something" to them.
  6. Describe this room to someone outside. "Imagine you're describing this room to a friend over the phone. What would you say first?" One or two volunteers answer out loud, and the rest just think it.

If a member can't do the task today, normalize it in a sentence. "Some days your brain just won't do the math. That's fine. Just look around the room instead."

Why to limit breathwork

Breathing exercises are often the first thing facilitators reach for. In a mixed group, they deserve more caution than they usually get.

  • The breath isn't neutral for everyone. Trauma-sensitive mindfulness teaching points out that attention on breathing can raise distress for some survivors. A member with panic may notice a fast heartbeat and feel worse, not better.
  • Breath holding and fast breathing can make people dizzy. Skip holds, counted breath retention and anything forceful. That matters more in a group where some members are on new medications.
  • You can't watch everyone's response. One-on-one, you'd notice if breath focus was making things worse. With nine people, you probably won't.

If you do use breath, make it brief and optional, and keep eyes open. A light version: "If you'd like, notice one breath going out. Or just notice one sound in the room." Then move on. Don't make breathing the only anchor, and don't use it as the default opener for a group with new members every week.

Choosing by room state

The right exercise depends less on the topic and more on how the room feels when you walk in. Here is a quick way to match them.

  • Anxious or keyed up (talking fast, fidgeting, glancing at the door): outward-focus exercises with a clear structure, such as colors in the room (1) or room alphabet (6). Concrete tasks give restless attention somewhere to go.
  • Sedated or low-energy (slow responses, heavy eyes): light cognitive games with no pressure, such as categories in your head (15), plus a small posture shift (14). Keep instructions short and repeat them once.
  • Members seeming far away or spaced out: orienting exercises, such as name the date and place (7) or sounds near and far (4). Use a slightly brighter voice, say members' names when you invite them back, and keep eyes open. If a member can't come back to the room, follow your program's procedures.
  • Mixed room with new members: start with the most neutral options, such as count the corners (2) or describe it like a detective (3). New members learn what to expect from what you do first. For more on that first day, see how to run a rolling-admission therapy group.
  • After a heavy disclosure, mid-session: a short, outward reset like the window or wall view (8), then a clear transition back to the plan.

Here's how Dana uses this in practice. On a fictional Monday, the room is quiet and members look tired after the weekend. She skips the breathing exercise she planned and uses categories in your head instead: "Silently, as many kinds of fruit as you can in 30 seconds. No sharing unless you want to." Two members smile at the end, and one says "mango" out loud. That's enough. The group has landed, and she moves into the session.

Keep a short list of five or six go-to exercises you know by heart. Rotate them so returning members don't get bored, and keep one outward-focus option ready for any day the room surprises you.
