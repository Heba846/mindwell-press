Day program, IOP, PHP: the same group under different names

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 17, 2026 · Editorial standards

A day program, a day patient program, an acute day unit, a day hospital, an IOP and a PHP are largely the same level of care under different national names: structured treatment during the day, delivered mostly in groups, with people going home at night. The names follow funding and local history, not what happens in the group room.

The same service, four names

There is a level of care that sits between a weekly appointment and an inpatient bed: people come in for structured treatment during the day, mostly in groups, and go home at night. Almost every health system has one. Almost none of them call it the same thing.

The naming follows funding and local history rather than clinical content. An Australian mental health services atlas makes the point directly, noting the wide variability in what services are called even within one geographical area, and the lack of relationship between a service’s name and what it actually does.

That matters practically. A facilitator searching for session material under her own local term will miss most of what exists, because the largest body of published material uses American vocabulary.

United StatesIntensive outpatient program (IOP) and partial hospitalization program (PHP); also day treatment or day hospital
England (NHS)Acute day unit; the same service was historically called a day hospital
Wales (NHS)Day care facility — locally a day hospital, day centre or day unit
AustraliaDay program or day patient program

Each of these names is sourced in its own section below.

United States: IOP, PHP, day hospital

American services are usually named by intensity. A partial hospitalization program is the more intensive of the two, and an intensive outpatient program the lighter one. Both are outpatient: people attend for a set number of hours and sleep at home.

Even inside the US, the same program carries more than one name. Sheppard Pratt, for example, describes its adult day hospital as also being called a partial hospitalization program, running group-based treatment Monday to Friday from about 8 a.m. to 3 p.m., with most people attending for two to three weeks.

The academic literature treats the terms as interchangeable too, describing psychiatric day hospitals, partial hospital programmes and day treatment programmes together as time-limited active treatment programmes offering intensive, coordinated and structured clinical services.

United Kingdom: acute day units and day hospitals

England does not use IOP or PHP as service names. The closest equivalents are acute day units, which the research literature notes were previously known as day hospitals, and which exist in some NHS Trusts as an alternative to inpatient admission. A national survey of them found no NHS-specified model: provision varies by Trust, and units typically offer a mix of individual and group sessions during the day, with people returning home overnight.

The naming is loose at the administrative level as well. The NHS data dictionary for Wales defines a day care facility as one that may be called a day hospital, a centre or a unit, and specifies that these facilities have no hospital beds and operate separately from any ward.

So a UK facilitator looking for group material will rarely type IOP or PHP, and material titled that way will rarely reach her.

Australia: day programs and day patient programs

Australian services, particularly in the private sector, most often use day program or day patient program. Perth Clinic lists day patient programs alongside its inpatient group options. The Banyans in Brisbane runs a mental health day program on an outpatient basis. Hyson Green in Canberra offers both day and inpatient programs.

One detail in the Australian material is worth a facilitator’s attention. Hyson Green describes its day patient programs as including both ongoing, open groups and fixed-period, closed ones. That distinction — open versus closed — is the one that actually determines how you run a session, and it is being made under a completely different service name from the American one.

What is the same everywhere

Across all of these names, three things hold:

  1. The group is the treatment, not an add-on to individual therapy. Most of the clinical hours happen in a room with other people in it.
  2. People arrive and leave on different days. Wherever a service admits people when they need care rather than filling a cohort, a single session has to work for someone on their first day and someone in their fourth week simultaneously.
  3. The facilitator absorbs the mismatch. When the printed plan does not fit the room, the adjustment happens live, in front of everyone, in the first minute.

These are facilitation problems, not jurisdictional ones. A grounding exercise does not become a different exercise because the service is funded differently.

What genuinely differs

The parts that do not travel are the administrative ones:

  • Hours and intensity thresholds, which are set by payers or health systems rather than by clinical convention.
  • Referral and entry routes: who can refer, whether a GP or psychiatrist referral is required, and whether private cover or public funding decides access.
  • Documentation requirements, including what a progress note must contain and how often a treatment plan is reviewed.
  • Level-of-care criteria governing who is admitted, who steps down and when.

These are local, and no published resource can settle them for you. Follow your own service’s policy and your regulator’s requirements.

Using group material written for another country

Most published group-therapy material is written in American vocabulary, because the American system names and funds this level of care most explicitly. If you work elsewhere, the practical approach is to separate the session from its wrapper.

The session itself — the teaching point, the activity, the processing questions, the closing — is usually portable, because the skills being taught are not country-specific. The wrapper is not: billing codes, required note formats, level-of-care language and prescribed hours should be treated as placeholders and replaced with your own.

The one thing worth checking before you adopt any curriculum is whether it was written for a cohort or for an open group. That single structural question matters far more than which country the author was writing in.

What to search for instead

If local vocabulary is not returning useful results, search the structure of the group rather than the name of the service. Terms that cross borders include open group, rolling admission, continuous entry, psychoeducation group, group facilitation and group therapy activities for adults. Terms that do not travel well include IOP, PHP and partial hospitalization, which will mostly surface US-specific material.

A resource that describes running one session for members at different stages of treatment on the same day is built for your job, whatever the service on its cover is called.

Common questions

Is a day program the same as an IOP or a PHP?

Not exactly, but they overlap heavily. In the US, a partial hospitalization program is often called a day hospital or day treatment, and research describes psychiatric day hospitals, partial hospital programmes and day treatment programmes as the same kind of time-limited, structured treatment. In Australia the common term is day program or day patient program. In England, acute day units were previously called day hospitals. The names follow funding and local history more than they follow what happens in the room.

What do UK services call an intensive outpatient program?

There is no single UK equivalent of the US term. England has acute day units, which were previously known as day hospitals, and researchers note there is no NHS-specified model for them. The NHS data dictionary for Wales says a day care facility may be called a day hospital, a centre or a unit, and that these have no hospital beds and operate separately from any ward.

What is a day patient program in Australia?

Australian private mental health services commonly run day programs or day patient programs alongside inpatient care. Some run both ongoing open groups and fixed-period closed groups. An Australian mental health services atlas notes that service names vary widely even within one area and often do not describe what the service actually does, so read the structure rather than the label.

Does the group itself change if the program has a different name?

The mechanics change less than the paperwork does. If members join and leave on different days, you are running an open or rolling-admission group, and the facilitation problem is the same anywhere: a session has to work for someone on their first day and someone in their fourth week at the same time. What does change is hours, funding route, referral pathway and documentation requirements, which are set locally.

Are group materials written for IOP and PHP usable in a day program?

The session content usually transfers, because the clinical skills taught are the same. What does not transfer is anything tied to a specific payer or regulator: billing codes, required documentation wording, level-of-care criteria and hours. Treat those parts as placeholders and replace them with your own service's requirements and your program's policy.

What should I search for if I work in a day program and want group material?

Search the structure, not the level-of-care name. Terms like open group, rolling admission, continuous entry, psychoeducation group and group facilitation will find material that fits, while IOP and PHP will mostly return US-specific results. If a resource describes running a session for members at different stages on the same day, it is built for the same job you are doing.
