How to handle a group member who monopolizes the session

By Heba El Garhy · Published October 25, 2026 · Editorial standards

Check-in was supposed to take eight minutes. One member has been talking for five, and two people haven't had a turn. A group member monopolizing group therapy time is one of the most common problems facilitators bring to supervision. Here is what may be driving it, what to say in the moment, and how to set up the group so you need to interrupt less often.

The examples below come from an adult IOP group with rolling admission: eight members, a 50-minute session, two members on their first day. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional.

Why a member may take over the airtime

You usually can't tell from the outside why someone keeps talking, so treat these as possibilities, not conclusions:

  • An urgent need. Something happened last night, and the member is afraid a short check-in means nobody will hear it.
  • Anxiety in the room. Talking can be a way to manage the discomfort of a quiet group, a new face, or a topic that feels close to home.
  • An unclear format. If nobody said how long a check-in answer should be, a member who answers at length is following a different rule, not breaking yours.
  • Difficulty organizing a story. Some members can't tell the short version yet. Stress, fatigue and some medications can make this harder.
  • A pattern the group can help with. In a process group, how a member takes up space can become useful material, once the group is ready for it and the member is not the only one under the light.

None of these is a reason to let one person use most of the session. Other members lose their turn, new members learn that check-in means long stories, and the member who monopolizes can end up resented by the group. A short, kind boundary protects that member too.

The three-part redirect

Most effective redirections have three parts. Keep the whole thing under 20 seconds.

  1. Acknowledge. Name something real about what the member said or how it seems to feel. "That sounds like a hard night." Skip praise you don't mean.
  2. Name the limit and the reason. Make the reason about the group's time, not the member's behavior. "I want to make sure everyone gets a turn before we start the skill."
  3. Offer a next step. Give the member somewhere for the rest of it to go: later in group, a staff check-in, or individual time. "Can we come back to this after the skill, or would a staff check-in help more?"

Use the member's name, keep your tone even, and look at them, then at the next person. Turning toward the next speaker is often enough on its own. If you're hesitant to interrupt, notice that waiting longer usually makes the interruption feel bigger when it comes.

What to say: by group type

Pick the line that sounds like you. These are alternatives, not escalating warnings.

During check-in

"Sam, I can hear a lot happened. I'm going to pause you there so everyone gets a turn. If you'd like, we can make space for it after the skill, or I can check what staff support is available. Who's next?"

If the member keeps going, repeat the same message in fewer words. Don't add new reasons. "Sam, I need to pause you. We'll come back to it."

During a skills group

"That's a good example of why this skill matters. Let's hold the details for now and use a practice example nobody has to share. Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet. You're welcome to pass."

Bring the group back to the planned skill with a fictional example instead of using the member's story as the demonstration. It protects their privacy and keeps the lesson moving.

During a process group

"Before we go further, I'd like to check in with the room. What's it been like to listen for the last few minutes? Anyone who hasn't spoken, you're welcome to come in, or pass."

In a process group, the room's reaction can be part of the work. Keep it about the group's experience, not a verdict on one person, and only open this door if you have time to close it well.

When the member is giving advice to others

"Thanks for wanting to help. Let's check with Alex first. Alex, would advice be useful right now, or would you rather just be heard?"

If the member objects

A member may say, "You're cutting me off," or "Nobody else had anything to say." Respond to the impact without reopening the whole story:

  1. Acknowledge it. "It's frustrating to be stopped mid-thought. I get that." If your interruption was abrupt, own it briefly.
  2. Hold the limit. "I still need to give others a turn."
  3. Repeat the next step. "Let's talk after group about what would help."

If the content suggests an urgent need, such as somewhere to stay tonight, or a safety concern, stop routine redirection and follow your program's support or safety procedures. A redirect should never be the reason an urgent need waits until next week.

Prevent it with structure, not confrontation

The best way to handle monopolizing is to need fewer interruptions in the first place.

  • Set the length before the first answer. "One word," "one number," or "one sentence." Then model it yourself with a short answer.
  • Use formats with a built-in limit. Rating scales, either/or choices, and group-level polls are hard to monopolize. See 60 group therapy check-in questions for options sorted by length.
  • Name airtime as a group norm. "We share airtime so everyone gets a turn" sits alongside confidentiality and the right to pass. Repeat it when a new member joins.
  • Agree on a cue privately. For a member who knows they talk at length, a small agreed signal, like a hand on the table, lets you redirect without stopping the room. Agree on it outside group, never announce it publicly.
  • Make space for longer sharing somewhere else. If a member has a lot to say every day, they may need more individual time. That's a treatment-team conversation, not a group problem.

In rolling-admission groups, structure matters even more. New members watch the first check-in to learn the rules. For more on this, see how to run a rolling-admission therapy group.

When it may be clinical

Sometimes a member who can't stop talking is showing a change that needs clinical attention rather than a better redirect. Watch for speech that is faster, louder or harder to interrupt than usual for this person, jumping between unrelated topics, unusual irritability, or signs of intoxication or confusion. These are observations to pass on, not a diagnosis to make in the room.

If you notice them, follow your program's assessment and response procedures. If you are leading alone, request support through your program's route and keep the group covered; don't leave the room unattended.

How to document it

Describe what happened and what you did. Avoid labels like "monopolizing" or "attention-seeking" in the note. Example excerpt (fictional, not a complete note):

"During check-in, client spoke for approximately five minutes about a recent family conflict. Facilitator acknowledged the concern, redirected to allow other members a turn, and offered a staff check-in after group. Client accepted and participated in the skills portion by observing the facilitator's practice example."

Keep other members' identities and disclosures out of the note. More examples: group therapy progress notes in GIRP and DAP.

After group

If it was a one-off, a brief "How did that land for you today?" is often enough. If it's a pattern across several sessions, raise it privately and with curiosity. Ask what the member needs from check-in, whether a cue would help, and whether more individual time would make group easier. Bring persistent patterns to your treatment team and supervision.

And if the opposite happens, and nobody talks at all, see what to do when nobody talks in group therapy.
