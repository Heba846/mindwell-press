Group therapy termination activities for IOP and PHP: saying goodbye when the group goes on

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 10, 2026 · Editorial standards

Group therapy termination activities in an open IOP or PHP group have two jobs: mark one member's last day, and keep everyone who stays in the room engaged. Ask the departing member first how they want to leave. Then use a short activity, under ten minutes, that gives every member a turn and ends on the group, not the goodbye.

The examples below come from an imagined adult IOP group with rolling admission. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional. These activities are facilitation ideas, not evidence-based interventions. Programs vary, so follow your program's policy on discharge, aftercare and what may be shared in group.

Why is termination different when the group continues?

In a closed group, everyone ends together. In an open IOP or PHP group, one person ends and everyone else comes back tomorrow. The goodbye has to work for the member who is leaving and for the members who are not.

That changes almost every choice you make. A closed group can spend a whole final session on review and farewell. An open group can't, because a member who started on Monday needs Thursday's skill session to run as planned.

Closed groupEveryone leaves on the same day. The last session can be all goodbye. There is no one left behind to think about.
Open groupOne member leaves while others stay. The goodbye is a few minutes inside a normal session. The members who stay matter as much as the member who leaves.

Departures are also frequent. In a busy rolling-admission group, someone may finish every week. If each goodbye takes half a session, the group spends more time ending than working. Short and repeatable beats long and moving.

Federal treatment guidance describes the same pressure. SAMHSA's TIP 41, Substance Abuse Treatment: Group Therapy, notes "the adverse effect that membership changes can have on group cohesion" in ongoing groups. It also says that when a senior member leaves, "another ordinarily will assume the role just vacated." A good goodbye helps that handover happen in the open.

The American Group Psychotherapy Association's practice guidelines put it more simply: "With each departure, the group becomes a new group, and the prior group is gone." Your job is to help the new group start well. For the structure behind all of this, see how to run a rolling-admission therapy group.

Why should you ask the departing member first?

Because the goodbye belongs to the member who is leaving. Some people want a moment with the group. Some would rather slip out quietly. Asking first is the only way to know, and it gives the member one more real choice before they go.

Ask privately, a session or two before the last day if you can. Keep it short. A fictional facilitator, Marisol, says it this way:

"Your last group here is Friday. People like to finish in different ways. Some want a few minutes with the group, some want a quiet mention, and some would rather just leave. What would feel right for you?"

Offer a simple menu so the member doesn't have to invent an answer:

  • A quiet exit. You tell the group at the end that today is the member's last day. Nothing more.
  • A short closing. Three to five minutes at the end of the session, using one activity below.
  • Notice ahead. The group hears a few sessions early, so members have time to say what they want to say.

Notice is worth offering when the member is willing. TIP 41 suggests that soon-to-depart members of an ongoing group "should give ample advance notice (perhaps 4 weeks)" so the group can work through the departure. IOP and PHP stays are often too short for that, and many discharge dates are set late. A few days' notice still helps.

Agree on what the group will hear. The member is finishing. That's all. The reason, the next level of care, insurance details and diagnosis stay out of the room unless the member chooses to share something about themselves, within your program's rules.

Not every departure is planned. A member may stop coming, be admitted elsewhere or leave early against advice. TIP 41 says groups "should be forewarned that sudden changes may take place." Say this when you review ground rules, so an empty chair is not a shock. If an unplanned departure raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures.

What are good goodbye activities that include staying members?

The best goodbye activities give every member a turn and end on the group, not on the person leaving. Each one below takes three to ten minutes. Say before you start that anyone can pass. For six more discharge closings, see the discharge section of group therapy closing activities.

  1. The opening note. If the member agrees, they take the first check-in and say, "Today's my last day." The group can respond in one sentence each, or not at all. Then you run the planned session as usual. This works well for members who want the group to know but don't want to be the topic.
  2. The handover. Every group has informal roles: the person who notices when someone is quiet, the one who asks the first question, the one who sets out the chairs. The departing member names one role they have played, in general terms, and asks who might pick it up. Staying members can volunteer or just think about it. It turns a loss into a job the group keeps.
  3. The skill relay. The departing member names one skill from group they plan to keep using. Then each staying member names the skill they would pick if today were their last day. Newer members can say, "I don't know yet," which is a real answer. Everyone leaves with a skill in mind.
  4. Then and now, for everyone. Each member, not only the one leaving, names one small difference between their first week and today. Keep it general: "I can sit through the whole group now." Members in their first week say what they hope will be different. It gives staying members a turn to notice their own progress.
  5. The one-line card. Pass around a blank card. Each member writes one line for the departing member, or a check mark if they'd rather not write. The facilitator reads it over before handing it on. Offer a spoken option for anyone who doesn't write, and check your program's policy on personal items and contact details first: no phone numbers, social media handles or last names.
  6. The empty-chair check. This one happens at the next session. Take 60 seconds to say, "Dana finished on Friday. Does anyone want to say something about that before we start?" It reaches members who missed the goodbye, and it gives the room permission to notice the change. Use it after unplanned departures too, with a factual sentence and nothing more.

Fictional example. Theo is finishing on Thursday after five weeks. He asks for "something small." Marisol uses the skill relay in the last eight minutes. Theo names "the two-minute rule for getting out of bed." A member in her second week says, "I don't know yet, maybe that one." A long-time member says opposite action. Marisol closes with, "That's three skills in this room that are still going." Then she ends on time.

Keep the ending on the group. Whatever activity you use, the last words should be about the people who are coming back. A sentence like "See everyone on Monday, and there'll be someone new in that chair" does that well.

How do you handle sadness, envy or anxiety in the room?

Name the feelings as normal, in general terms, and don't let any one of them take over the session. A discharge can stir up a mix of reactions in the members who stay. Most of them make sense, and none of them needs to be fixed in the moment.

The AGPA guidelines call loss "a core aspect of the experience of termination," for both the members who leave and the members who remain. They also note that remaining members can easily feel apprehensive about their own progress. You will see some version of each of these:

Sadness"I'm going to miss him." Accept it and reflect it briefly. Missing someone is a sign the group mattered.
Envy"Must be nice to be done." Say that it's common to feel glad and a little left behind at the same time. Don't argue with it or correct it.
Anxiety"I've been here longer and I'm not leaving." Remind the room that stays differ in length for many reasons, and that a discharge date is not a score.
Holding on"You should stay another week." TIP 41 notes that "it is natural for individuals and groups to try to hold onto each other." Thank the member for caring, and keep the decision where it belongs, with the departing member and the treatment team.
Relief or no reactionSome members feel nothing much, or are glad of more airtime. That's fine too. Don't ask anyone to perform a feeling.

A short script covers most of it. Marisol uses this one:

"When someone finishes, people feel all kinds of things. Glad, sad, a bit left behind, or nothing much. All of that is normal here. Everyone's stay is its own length."

If one member's reaction is strong, acknowledge it and offer a private check-in after group rather than processing it at length in the room. If anything a member says raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures.

What should you not say when a member leaves group?

Avoid anything that ranks progress, reveals details or puts the departing member on the spot. Most slips come from warmth, not carelessness. A quick swap fixes them.

"Congratulations on graduating!"It implies everyone else hasn't. Try: "Today is Theo's last group with us."
"See, if you work hard, you get out too."It turns a discharge into a reward and staying into a failure. Try: "Everyone's stay is its own length."
"Where are you going next?"It invites discharge details in front of the group. Let the member share only what they choose.
"It won't be the same without you."It hands the member a burden on the way out and tells the room it is now worse. Try: "We'll notice you're not here."
"Come back and visit anytime."It may break program policy and blurs the boundary. Point to the aftercare supports your program offers instead.
"Don't come back here, okay?"It's meant kindly, but it can feel like shame to anyone who returns later. Try: "We hope the next step goes well."

Also skip long speeches from staff. A two-sentence goodbye from the facilitator is plenty. The goodbye works best when it comes from the group.

What should you document about a member's last group?

Document the departing member's last session the way you document any group session, following your program's policy. Record what you observed and what you delivered. Note that it was the member's last scheduled group if your program asks for that.

Keep other members out of it. Each member's note records their own participation, so staying members' reactions belong in their own notes, if anywhere, and not in the departing member's record. Discharge summaries and aftercare plans follow your program's own process.

If a member left without notice, record the facts you know, such as missed sessions, and follow your program's procedures for follow-up. For the basics of writing a group note, see how to write group therapy progress notes.

Common questions

What are good termination activities for an open therapy group?

Good termination activities in an open group are short and give every member a turn, not only the person leaving. Try a skill relay, where the departing member names a skill they will keep and staying members name the one they would pick, or a handover, where the departing member passes an informal role to the group. Ask the member first and keep it under ten minutes.

Should I tell the group before a member's last day?

Ask the departing member first, then follow their choice and your program's policy. Some members want the group told a few sessions ahead so there is time to say goodbye. Others prefer a short mention on the day. Either way, share only that the member is finishing, never the reason, the next level of care or other discharge details.

How do I handle a member who leaves the group without saying goodbye?

Give the group a short, factual acknowledgement at the next session, such as "Sam is no longer in this group, and I can't share more than that." Then invite one round of reactions, with a right to pass, and return to the planned session. Don't guess at the reason in the room. If the departure raises a safety concern, follow your program's procedures.

What if staying members feel jealous or worried when someone is discharged?

Treat those feelings as normal and name them for the room in general terms: some people feel glad, some feel left behind, and both make sense. Remind members that stays differ in length for many reasons and that a discharge date is not a score. Then give staying members something forward-looking to say about their own next step.

Can group members exchange phone numbers on a member's last day?

Follow your program's policy, since programs differ on contact between current and former members. Many have rules about it, so check before the last day rather than during the goodbye. If contact is not allowed, say so warmly and plainly, and point the departing member to the aftercare supports your program offers instead.
