What a group progress note is, and what it isn't

A group progress note records one member's participation in one group session: what the group was for, what you delivered, how this person responded, and what happens next. The group is shared. The note belongs to one person's record.

It is not the same as what HIPAA calls psychotherapy notes. Under the Privacy Rule, psychotherapy notes are a mental health professional's notes documenting or analyzing the contents of a private, group, joint or family counseling session, kept separate from the rest of the medical record. The definition excludes, among other things, medication prescription and monitoring, session start and stop times, the modalities and frequency of treatment, results of clinical tests, and summaries of diagnosis, functional status, treatment plan, symptoms, prognosis and progress. Those belong in the record, and that is where a group progress note lives. Write it knowing the member may read it, other clinicians will rely on it, and reviewers may audit it.

GIRP vs DAP: the two formats

Both formats hold the same information in a different order. Use the one your program requires. If your program uses BIRP or SOAP instead, see the same session written in all four formats.

GIRPGoal from this member's treatment plan · Intervention you delivered · Response: what you observed and what the member reported · Plan or next step. GIRP starts from the person's goal, which suits skills and psychoeducation groups where each session targets a defined skill.
DAPData: what was delivered and what you observed or the member reported · Assessment: your clinical interpretation, tied to the goal · Plan. DAP gives interpretation its own section, which helps keep it separate from observation.

You may also see SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) and BIRP (Behavior, Intervention, Response, Plan). The principles below apply to all of them.

What every group note needs

Required fields are set by your program, your state and the payer, so check your own documentation policy first. As one public example, a group therapy notes tip sheet posted on sf.gov, summarizing California Medi-Cal (DHCS) requirements, lists the service type and date, the client's location, the provider's name and signature, a narrative of how the service addressed the client's need, the client's response, and next steps. Across settings, most group notes cover:

  1. The basics: date, group type and code, start and stop time, format (in person, video, hybrid), and the clinician.
  2. The goal: the specific goal from this member's plan that the group addressed, not a generic group objective.
  3. What you delivered: the skill or topic, the activity, and any accommodation or alternative route you offered.
  4. This member's response: what you observed, separately from what they said. Include partial participation, watching, passing and leaving early.
  5. The plan: a step the member chose, or a clear "no step chosen", plus any follow-up and who owns it.

Keep other members out of the note

Don't write another member's name, initials or disclosures in this person's record. "Member responded supportively when a peer shared about a recent loss" says what you need without exposing the peer. The sf.gov tip sheet is explicit on this point for its setting: other clients are not named in a client's record, and the participant list is kept outside individual charts.

If one member's behavior affected another, describe your observation of this member's behavior and your intervention: "Member interrupted others three times; facilitator restated the speaking-turn agreement; member waited for turns for the rest of the session."

Three worked examples

Same group for the first two: an adult IOP skills group, 90 minutes, rolling admission. The session taught the thought-feeling-behavior chain and common thinking traps. All names and details are invented, and these are excerpts, not complete notes.

1. A returning member who practiced (GIRP)

G: Notice links between thoughts, feelings and actions when work stress rises (member's plan).

I: Taught the thought-feeling-behavior chain and five common thinking traps; led a group chain on a made-up example, then optional personal practice with a moderate example. Worksheet offered with a no-writing option.

R: Member completed a chain aloud on a recent work email and identified "mind reading" in the thought "My manager thinks I'm incompetent." Stated the chain was "easier to see written down." Asked how to use it when anxious.

P: Member chose to note one thinking trap per day on the worksheet. Facilitator to review at next skills group.

2. A first-day member who only watched (DAP)

D: First group attended. Same session as above; member received a brief newcomer orientation. Member watched the group example, did not complete a personal chain, and stated, "I'd rather listen today." Asked what "thinking trap" means; facilitator answered.

A: Engaged as an observer during first group; understanding of the skill not yet assessed. Question suggests interest in the core concept. No safety concerns observed or reported during group; no risk screening conducted in group.

P: No between-session step chosen. Offer the entry version of the worksheet at the next skills group; primary clinician informed of first-group attendance per program routine.

Notice what this note doesn't say: "resistant", "unmotivated" or "participated well". Watching is recorded as watching.

3. A process group (GIRP, short)

G: Reduce isolation by sharing one current concern with others (member's plan).

I: Facilitated a process group on hope and doubt in treatment; invited optional sharing and peer feedback; restated the group agreement on privacy.

R: Member shared, without detail, that "the weekends are the hardest." Responded to one peer's feedback with "That makes sense." Stated afterward that it was "strange but OK" to say it aloud.

P: Member chose to name one weekend contact in their own support plan with their primary clinician this week.

Writing notes for a rolling-admission group

In an open-enrollment IOP or PHP group, the same session holds someone on day one and someone in week three. The note should show which route this member took, because the same session means different things to each of them.

  • First day: record orientation and the entry route offered. Don't record the skill as learned.
  • Returning: record application to a current situation, or helping a newer member, if that's what happened.
  • Familiarity isn't mastery. "Member stated they have done this skill before" is a report. Record practice only when it was demonstrated or reported, and say which.

Common mistakes

  • The same note for everyone. Copying one paragraph into eight charts with only the name changed. The intervention line can be shared; the response and plan can't.
  • Labels instead of observations. "Resistant", "non-compliant" or "in denial" say more about the writer than the member. Describe what happened.
  • Attendance written as progress. "Member attended and participated" doesn't show anything about the goal.
  • Interpretation hiding in the data. In DAP, keep "member appeared anxious" in the Assessment, with the observation that supports it ("member's leg was bouncing; member stated, 'I'm on edge today'") in the Data.
  • A vague plan. "Continue group" is a schedule, not a plan. Name the step, or note that none was chosen, and who follows up.
  • Leaving out risk because group moved on. If something raised a safety concern, document what you observed, what you did and who you handed it to, according to your program's procedure. The progress note doesn't replace that procedure.

What do auditors look for in group therapy progress notes?

Auditors check whether each note proves that the billed service happened, was medically necessary, was individual to this member and ties to this member's plan goals. The US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) reports that insufficient documentation accounted for 78.3% of improper payments for psychiatry services in its 2024 reporting period. The note is the evidence.

Most of what follows is US payer rules, and it is labelled that way. If you work in a day programme outside the US, read the last paragraph of this section first.

What does a US Medicare auditor check in a group note?

US Medicare's billing and coding article for psychiatric partial hospitalization programs (A56850) requires a separate progress note for each service rendered. For group therapy it says the documentation must indicate the name of the group and the group type, and it must also record the material discussed and the patient's response. The table turns those requirements into a checklist.

What the auditor checksWhat the note must therefore show
Each billed service has its own noteA separate note for each service rendered, in this member's record, not one summary for the room.
The service was a group, and which oneThe name of the group and the group type, for example "Skills group" or "Process group".
What the service containedThe material discussed or taught, in plain words that say what happened in the session.
This member's responseThe patient's response to the intervention: what you observed, what the member reported, including watching or passing.
The link to the planHow that response relates to the patient's long or short term goals. This is where a GIRP "G" does its work.
The note is authenticatedLegible, dated and signed, with the credentials of the rendering provider.
The record supports medical necessityThe chart as a whole fully supports the medical necessity of the services, and the plan states the type, amount, frequency and duration of each service.

The last row combines two sources. The medical-necessity wording is from A56850. The treatment plan wording is from CMS's Medicare Learning Network compliance tips for outpatient psychiatric care, which also says the plan must clearly justify the need for each therapy and explain how it fits into the patient's treatment.

What does an audit sample actually cite?

In one US federal audit of one state's Medicaid adult partial care claims, 92 of 100 sampled claims did not comply with requirements. The HHS Office of Inspector General (report A-02-13-01029, December 2016, New Jersey) found 19 of those 92 claims had more than one deficiency. This is one state, one program and one audit, so treat it as evidence of what reviewers sample for, not as a national rule.

The deficiencies it listed were services not documented or supported, services not provided by or under the direction of a psychiatrist, individualized plan-of-care requirements not met, weekly progress notes not documented, and the provider not having an applicable intake assessment. Four of the five are about the chart around the note: the plan, the intake, the supervision and the note's existence.

Why do identical notes matter in a rolling-admission group?

In a rolling-admission group, ten people sit through the same session at different points in treatment, so the intervention is shared and the note's job is to show what was different for this member. A note that could be pasted into any chart does not show a response or a goal link for this person, and those are the two elements the Medicare article asks for.

The sources above do not discuss copied notes by name, so this is the reasoning behind the checklist rather than a quoted rule. The practical fix is to share the intervention line and write the response and plan for one person: a day-one member's response is orientation and watching, and a week-three member's is applying the skill to a current situation. The rolling-admission section shows both routes. To do it without writing about other members, use the approach in Keep other members out of the note: describe your observation of this member and what you did.

Which dates and signatures does an auditor look for?

Under US Medicare's partial hospitalization rules, the key dates are the initial psychiatric evaluation, the first recertification and the treatment plan review. Your program tracks these, and your progress note is where the evidence lands.

  • Initial psychiatric evaluation: A56850 says it must be performed and placed in the chart within 48 hours of admission.
  • First recertification: required as of the 18th day of partial hospitalization services, with later recertifications at provider-set intervals no less often than every 30 days, and signed by a physician who is treating the patient.
  • Treatment plan review: goals must be measurable, functional, time-framed and tied to the reason for admission, and the plan is reviewed never less often than every 31 days.

These are US Medicare PHP rules. An IOP, a Medicaid program or a private payer may use different intervals, so use your own program's schedule.

What does a note that fails a sample look like, and how do you fix it?

A note fails when it could describe any member of any group. This example is fictional, and the group, the member and the details are invented. First, the note as it might be found in a sample.

Fictional note that fails: "Member attended group and participated well. Group covered coping skills. Continue group." [no date, no signature]

It has no group name or type, no material, no response, no link to a goal, no plan and no authentication. "Participated well" and "continue group" describe attendance and a schedule, not this member's progress. The same session, rewritten for the same fictional member:

Fictional note that passes: 11/09/2026, 10:00 to 11:30. Skills group (adult IOP, in person).

G: Notice links between thoughts, feelings and actions when work stress rises (member's plan, short-term goal 2).

I: Taught the thought-feeling-behavior chain and five common thinking traps; led a group chain on a made-up example, then offered optional personal practice.

R: Member completed a chain aloud on a recent work email and identified "mind reading" in the thought "My manager thinks I'm incompetent." Stated the chain was "easier to see written down."

P: Member chose to note one thinking trap per day on the worksheet. Facilitator to review at next skills group.

[Legible signature, credentials, date]

Same session, same intervention line another member might share. The response and plan belong to one person, and the goal link is visible. Nothing in it names another member.

What if I work outside the US?

The CMS and OIG rules above are US payer rules and do not apply elsewhere. For England, regulation 17 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 requires providers to maintain securely an accurate, complete and contemporaneous record in respect of each service user, including a record of the care and treatment provided and of decisions taken about it.

The transferable point is that the principles an auditor tests are the same where the payer rules are not: the note is individual to one person, written at the time, tied to a plan, and signed. For Ireland, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, ask your programme for its own documentation standard, because this page does not cite one.

Whichever country you are in, follow your own program's policy and your payer's requirements. They decide the fields, the intervals and who signs.

ObservedMember completed… · demonstrated… · wrote… · watched the example · passed on the share · left at [time] · returned at [time]
ReportedMember stated… · described… · reported… · said, "…" · asked…
Not assessedUnderstanding not assessed · declined practice · did not share an example during the observed practice period
PlanMember chose to… · no between-session step chosen · facilitator to review at… · primary clinician informed per program routine

Common questions

What is the difference between GIRP and DAP notes?

Both formats hold the same information in a different order, so use the one your program requires. GIRP stands for Goal, Intervention, Response and Plan, and it starts from the goal in the member's treatment plan. DAP stands for Data, Assessment and Plan, and it gives your clinical interpretation its own section, which helps keep it separate from observation.

How do I write a progress note for a group member who only watched?

Record watching as watching. Note the orientation you gave, that the member watched the example, and anything they said or asked. State that understanding was not assessed, and record the between-session step or that none was chosen. Avoid labels such as resistant or unmotivated, and avoid phrases such as participated well, because they do not describe what happened.

Should I include other group members' names in a client's progress note?

No. Do not write another member's name, initials or disclosures in this person's record. Describe your observation of this member's behavior and your intervention instead, for example that the member responded supportively when a peer shared about a recent loss. Your program's documentation policy sets the final rules for what belongs in a chart.

Are group progress notes the same as HIPAA psychotherapy notes?

No. Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule, psychotherapy notes are a clinician's notes documenting or analyzing a counseling session, kept separate from the rest of the medical record. The definition excludes items such as session start and stop times and summaries of symptoms, treatment plan and progress. A group progress note lives in the record. This guide is educational, not legal advice.

What should every group therapy progress note include?

Required fields are set by your program, your state and the payer, so check your documentation policy first. Most group notes cover the basics (date, group type and code, times, format and clinician), the specific goal from this member's plan, what you delivered, this member's response, and a plan with a next step or a clear note that none was chosen, plus follow-up and who owns it.

What do auditors look for in group therapy progress notes?

Auditors check whether each note proves that the billed service happened, was medically necessary and was individual to that member. In US Medicare's partial hospitalization article, a group note must show the group name and type, the material discussed, the patient's response and the link to treatment goals, and be legible, dated and signed with credentials. Payer rules differ, so follow your program's policy.

Is it a problem if every group member's progress note is identical?

Yes, it is a risk. A note has to record this member's response and how it relates to this member's goals, and eight people rarely respond in the same way. The intervention line can be shared across the group, but the response and the plan should be written for one person. Identical response lines make it hard to show the service was individual. Follow your program's policy.

Does a group therapy progress note have to name the group and what was covered?

Under US Medicare's partial hospitalization billing article, yes. Documentation of group therapy sessions must indicate the name of the group and the group type, plus the material discussed and the patient's response. Other payers and other countries set their own fields, so check your program's policy and your payer's requirements before you change how you write notes.
