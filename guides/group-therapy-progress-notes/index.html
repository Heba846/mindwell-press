What a group progress note is, and what it isn't

A group progress note records one member's participation in one group session: what the group was for, what you delivered, how this person responded, and what happens next. The group is shared. The note belongs to one person's record.

It is not the same as what HIPAA calls psychotherapy notes. Under the Privacy Rule, psychotherapy notes are a mental health professional's notes documenting or analyzing the contents of a private, group, joint or family counseling session, kept separate from the rest of the medical record. The definition excludes, among other things, medication prescription and monitoring, session start and stop times, the modalities and frequency of treatment, results of clinical tests, and summaries of diagnosis, functional status, treatment plan, symptoms, prognosis and progress. Those belong in the record, and that is where a group progress note lives. Write it knowing the member may read it, other clinicians will rely on it, and reviewers may audit it.

GIRP vs DAP: the two formats

Both formats hold the same information in a different order. Use the one your program requires.

GIRPGoal from this member's treatment plan · Intervention you delivered · Response: what you observed and what the member reported · Plan or next step. GIRP starts from the person's goal, which suits skills and psychoeducation groups where each session targets a defined skill.
DAPData: what was delivered and what you observed or the member reported · Assessment: your clinical interpretation, tied to the goal · Plan. DAP gives interpretation its own section, which helps keep it separate from observation.

You may also see SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) and BIRP (Behavior, Intervention, Response, Plan). The principles below apply to all of them.

What every group note needs

Required fields are set by your program, your state and the payer, so check your own documentation policy first. As one public example, a group therapy notes tip sheet posted on sf.gov, summarizing California Medi-Cal (DHCS) requirements, lists the service type and date, the client's location, the provider's name and signature, a narrative of how the service addressed the client's need, the client's response, and next steps. Across settings, most group notes cover:

  1. The basics: date, group type and code, start and stop time, format (in person, video, hybrid), and the clinician.
  2. The goal: the specific goal from this member's plan that the group addressed, not a generic group objective.
  3. What you delivered: the skill or topic, the activity, and any accommodation or alternative route you offered.
  4. This member's response: what you observed, separately from what they said. Include partial participation, watching, passing and leaving early.
  5. The plan: a step the member chose, or a clear "no step chosen", plus any follow-up and who owns it.

Keep other members out of the note

Don't write another member's name, initials or disclosures in this person's record. "Member responded supportively when a peer shared about a recent loss" says what you need without exposing the peer. The sf.gov tip sheet is explicit on this point for its setting: other clients are not named in a client's record, and the participant list is kept outside individual charts.

If one member's behavior affected another, describe your observation of this member's behavior and your intervention: "Member interrupted others three times; facilitator restated the speaking-turn agreement; member waited for turns for the rest of the session."

Three worked examples

Same group for the first two: an adult IOP skills group, 90 minutes, rolling admission. The session taught the thought-feeling-behavior chain and common thinking traps. All names and details are invented, and these are excerpts, not complete notes.

1. A returning member who practiced (GIRP)

G: Notice links between thoughts, feelings and actions when work stress rises (member's plan).

I: Taught the thought-feeling-behavior chain and five common thinking traps; led a group chain on a made-up example, then optional personal practice with a moderate example. Worksheet offered with a no-writing option.

R: Member completed a chain aloud on a recent work email and identified "mind reading" in the thought "My manager thinks I'm incompetent." Stated the chain was "easier to see written down." Asked how to use it when anxious.

P: Member chose to note one thinking trap per day on the worksheet. Facilitator to review at next skills group.

2. A first-day member who only watched (DAP)

D: First group attended. Same session as above; member received a brief newcomer orientation. Member watched the group example, did not complete a personal chain, and stated, "I'd rather listen today." Asked what "thinking trap" means; facilitator answered.

A: Engaged as an observer during first group; understanding of the skill not yet assessed. Question suggests interest in the core concept. No safety concerns observed or reported during group; no risk screening conducted in group.

P: No between-session step chosen. Offer the entry version of the worksheet at the next skills group; primary clinician informed of first-group attendance per program routine.

Notice what this note doesn't say: "resistant", "unmotivated" or "participated well". Watching is recorded as watching.

3. A process group (GIRP, short)

G: Reduce isolation by sharing one current concern with others (member's plan).

I: Facilitated a process group on hope and doubt in treatment; invited optional sharing and peer feedback; restated the group agreement on privacy.

R: Member shared, without detail, that "the weekends are the hardest." Responded to one peer's feedback with "That makes sense." Stated afterward that it was "strange but OK" to say it aloud.

P: Member chose to name one weekend contact in their own support plan with their primary clinician this week.

Writing notes for a rolling-admission group

In an open-enrollment IOP or PHP group, the same session holds someone on day one and someone in week three. The note should show which route this member took, because the same session means different things to each of them.

  • First day: record orientation and the entry route offered. Don't record the skill as learned.
  • Returning: record application to a current situation, or helping a newer member, if that's what happened.
  • Familiarity isn't mastery. "Member stated they have done this skill before" is a report. Record practice only when it was demonstrated or reported, and say which.

Common mistakes

  • The same note for everyone. Copying one paragraph into eight charts with only the name changed. The intervention line can be shared; the response and plan can't.
  • Labels instead of observations. "Resistant", "non-compliant" or "in denial" say more about the writer than the member. Describe what happened.
  • Attendance written as progress. "Member attended and participated" doesn't show anything about the goal.
  • Interpretation hiding in the data. In DAP, keep "member appeared anxious" in the Assessment, with the observation that supports it ("member's leg was bouncing; member stated, 'I'm on edge today'") in the Data.
  • A vague plan. "Continue group" is a schedule, not a plan. Name the step, or note that none was chosen, and who follows up.
  • Leaving out risk because group moved on. If something raised a safety concern, document what you observed, what you did and who you handed it to, according to your program's procedure. The progress note doesn't replace that procedure.

Useful phrases: observed vs reported

ObservedMember completed… · demonstrated… · wrote… · watched the example · passed on the share · left at [time] · returned at [time]
ReportedMember stated… · described… · reported… · said, "…" · asked…
Not assessedUnderstanding not assessed · declined practice · did not share an example during the observed practice period
PlanMember chose to… · no between-session step chosen · facilitator to review at… · primary clinician informed per program routine
