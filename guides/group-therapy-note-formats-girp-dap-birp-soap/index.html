GIRP vs DAP vs BIRP vs SOAP notes for group therapy: one session, four formats

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 2, 2026 · Editorial standards

GIRP, DAP, BIRP and SOAP hold nearly the same information in a different order. For a group, the harder part is the same in all four: writing a note about one member, not about the room. Below, one fictional skills-group session is written in each format so you can compare them side by side.

The four formats at a glance

GIRPGoal · Intervention · Response · Plan. Starts from the member's treatment-plan goal, so every note ties back to why they are in the program.
DAPData · Assessment · Plan. Facts first (what you delivered, what you observed, what the member reported), then your clinical read, then next steps.
BIRPBehavior · Intervention · Response · Plan. Starts from what the member presented with in the session, then what you did and how they responded.
SOAPSubjective · Objective · Assessment · Plan. Separates what the member said from what you observed, then your assessment and plan.

All four end with a plan, and all four are meant to record what happened in the session, not a transcript of it.

How to choose

There is no universal standard, and in most programs you do not get to choose. Your program, your payer or your electronic record system sets the format. If you do have a choice, these are the practical differences:

  • GIRP keeps notes anchored to treatment-plan goals, which can help when a reviewer asks how a group connects to a member's plan.
  • DAP is short. Its "Data" section carries both what the member said and what you saw, so write the two apart clearly.
  • BIRP suits groups where what the member did in the room is the main event, such as a process group.
  • SOAP is the most familiar format in medical settings, and its Subjective/Objective split makes "reported" versus "observed" explicit.

Whichever you use, the skill is the same: write only about this member, say what was observed and what was reported, and tie the plan to what happens next.

The fictional session

This is an invented example for illustration, not a real person. An adult IOP skills group meets for 50 minutes. The skill is checking whether a strong emotion fits the facts of a situation. Member M. is in their second week. Their treatment-plan goal is to manage anger at work without leaving the situation. In group, M. completed the worksheet, described a recent disagreement with a supervisor, and needed one prompt to begin. M. rated the emotion's intensity 8 out of 10 before the exercise and 5 out of 10 after, and said they would try the skill before Thursday's meeting.

The same session in four formats

GIRP

Goal: Manage anger at work without leaving the situation (treatment plan goal 2). Intervention: 50-minute skills group. Facilitator taught the skill of checking whether an emotion fits the facts, led worksheet practice and prompted application to a recent situation. Response: Completed the worksheet after one prompt to begin. Described a disagreement with a supervisor. Self-rated emotion intensity 8/10 before and 5/10 after. Plan: Continue skills groups on the rotation. Member to try the skill before Thursday's meeting. Review its use at next session.

DAP

Data: Attended 50-minute skills group. Skill taught: checking whether an emotion fits the facts. Completed the worksheet after one prompt to begin. Described a disagreement with a supervisor. Self-rated intensity 8/10 before and 5/10 after. Stated plan to use the skill before Thursday's meeting. Assessment: Engaged with prompting. Reported short-term reduction in intensity after practice. Progress toward the work-anger goal is early and based on self-report. Plan: Continue skills groups. Review use of the skill at next session.

BIRP

Behavior: Arrived on time and began quietly. Needed one prompt to start the worksheet, then completed it and described a work disagreement to the group. Intervention: Facilitator taught the emotion-fits-the-facts skill, led worksheet practice and prompted application to a recent situation. Response: Self-rated intensity 8/10 before and 5/10 after. Stated intent to use the skill before Thursday's meeting. Plan: Continue skills groups. Review use at next session.

SOAP

Subjective: Member reported a disagreement with a supervisor, rated the emotion 8/10 before the exercise and 5/10 after, and said they would try the skill before Thursday's meeting. Objective: Attended 50-minute skills group. Began after one prompt. Completed the worksheet. Participated when invited. Assessment: Engaged with prompting. Early, self-reported benefit from practice, linked to the work-anger goal. Plan: Continue skills groups. Review use of the skill at next session.

The content did not change. Only the headings and the order did. Adapt all wording to your program's requirements and payer rules.

What stays the same in every format

  • One member per note. The group is shared, but each note belongs to one person's record. Do not copy the same paragraph across all eight members.
  • Keep other members out. Do not name peers or record what they disclosed.
  • Separate observed from reported. "Completed the worksheet" is observed. "Said the skill helped" is reported. Record both, and label which is which.
  • Describe silence plainly. A member who watched and did not speak gets a note too: what they did, what you offered, and what you plan next.

Mixed-tenure groups

In a rolling-admission group, the same session lands differently on a first-day member and a returning one. The format does not need to change, but the note should: a first-day member's note might record orientation and observation, while a returning member's might record application. Common questions

What is the difference between GIRP, DAP, BIRP and SOAP notes?

They hold nearly the same information in a different order. GIRP starts from the treatment goal, DAP from the data, BIRP from the member's behavior and SOAP from what the member said versus what you observed. All four end with a plan. Your program or payer usually decides which one you use.

Which note format is best for group therapy?

No format is universally best, and most programs set it for you. GIRP ties each note to a treatment-plan goal, DAP is short, BIRP suits process groups and SOAP makes reported and observed information explicit. Whatever you use, write one note per member, keep other members out of it and record what was observed.

How do I write a group note for a member who barely spoke?

Write what you observed and what you offered, without inferring more. For example, record that the member attended, followed the activity and declined to speak when invited, then state your plan. A note about a quiet member is still a note about that member's participation.

Can I copy the same note for every member of the group?

No. A group progress note belongs to one person's record and should describe that member's participation, response and plan. You can reuse the description of what the group did, but each member's response and plan should be written for that person.
