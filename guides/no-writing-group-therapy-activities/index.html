Group therapy activities for adults with mental illness: no-writing options for PHP and acute groups

By Heba El Garhy · Published November 17, 2026 · Editorial standards

The group therapy activities for adults with mental illness that hold up on a hard day ask for no writing at all. Use spoken rounds with a set length, point-to choices on a board, simple drawn or gesture answers, and skills taught from one spoken example. Every format keeps a real right to pass.

The examples below come from an imagined adult PHP group with rolling admission: nine members, a 45-minute skills session, one member on their first day. Every member and every line of dialogue is fictional. These activities are facilitation formats, not evidence-based interventions. Programs vary, so follow your program's policy on required forms, participation and safety.

Why do worksheets exclude some members?

A worksheet asks for reading, writing, focus and a steady hand at the same time. On a hard day, many members in PHP and acute groups can't do all four. The member who can't is often the one who most needs the skill.

The barriers are ordinary. Glasses left at home. A tremor or a sore hand. A reading level below the handout. Spelling the member is ashamed of. A first language that isn't English. Or a head that won't hold a sentence long enough to write it down.

Concentration is part of many diagnoses, not only a bad day. The National Institute of Mental Health's schizophrenia guide says cognitive symptoms "include problems with attention, concentration, and memory." It adds that these symptoms "can make it hard to follow a conversation, learn new things, or remember appointments." A fill-in-the-blank sheet leans on all three.

You often can't tell who is struggling by looking. The federal Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) makes the same point in its Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit: "we do not know who is challenged by health information and tasks at any given moment." The toolkit also notes that "even people who usually manage well can find it hard to take in information."

So make the no-writing route the default for the whole room, not an exception for one person. When everyone is offered "say it, point to it, or write it," nobody has to admit they can't write today. The member holding a pen and staring at a blank page stops being the only one not taking part.

Spoken formats

The safest spoken formats have a set length and a built-in pass. Tell members how long an answer should be before the first person speaks, then give your own short answer first.

  1. One-word round. "One word for how you arrived today. Pass is a word too." Model it: "Slow."
  2. Number round. "Zero to ten, how much energy do you have right now? Say the number, show it on your fingers, or pass." Numbers are easy to say and hard to get wrong.
  3. Finish the sentence. Give the stem and keep it small. "One thing that helped a little since yesterday was..." A member can finish it with one word.
  4. This or that. Either/or questions with no right answer and no disclosure: "Morning or evening?" "Loud room or quiet room?" They warm up voices without asking anyone to explain.
  5. Echo round. You say a skill step, and members repeat one key word from it. "When the urge shows up, we pause. What's the word?" Saying the word is the practice.
  6. You are the scribe. Members call out ideas and you write them on the board. "What are some things people do while they wait for an urge to pass?" The list belongs to the group, and nobody has to hold a pen.

Point-to formats

Point-to formats put the choices where everyone can see them, so members answer by pointing, holding up a card or nodding. They suit members who are too tired to find words, and members who don't want to speak yet.

  1. Energy board. Five large boxes on the whiteboard, from "running on empty" to "full tank," with a simple picture in each. "Point to where you are, or tell me the box number."
  2. Card pick. Lay out six to eight cards with one large word each, like "tired," "okay," "worried," "steady." Members pick up the card that fits, or leave them all.
  3. Board thermometer. Draw a tall line marked 0 at the bottom and 10 at the top. Members point to a height. You can mark the group's spread without names.
  4. Hands vote. Group-level questions with a show of hands: "Raise a hand if mornings are the hardest part of your day." Nobody is named, and keeping your hand down is a full answer.
  5. Pick the skill. Put three skill cards from this week on the table. "Point to the one you might try before tomorrow." It replaces the written plan at the bottom of most worksheets.
  6. Two-sides choice. Hold up two cards: "Easier than yesterday" and "Harder than yesterday." Members point left or right, or stay still.

Keep point-to materials simple. Use large print, one word plus a picture, and three to five options. Too many choices turns a low-demand task back into a reading test.

Drawn and gesture formats

Drawn and gesture formats let a member answer with a mark or a movement instead of words. Keep them optional, and never treat a member's drawing as something to interpret.

  1. Draw your weather. Hand out blank cards and markers. "Draw today's weather. Sun, cloud, rain, anything. Stick figures welcome." Members can show it, describe it, or keep it face down.
  2. One line for the week. "Draw one line showing how the week has gone. Up, down, flat or zigzag." It takes ten seconds and needs no skill.
  3. Color pick. Put out a handful of markers. "Pick a color for today. No need to explain it."
  4. Thumb scale. Thumbs up, sideways or down for a simple question: "How was getting here this morning?"
  5. Stand or stay. "If you'd like to, stand up and stretch. Staying seated is fine." It gives a heavy room a reset without asking anyone to talk.

This is not art therapy. If a member shows a drawing, ask "Want to say anything about it?" and accept "No." Don't read meaning into the colors or shapes, and don't let other members guess what it means.

How do you adapt a skills lesson with no handout?

Teach one skill in three steps or fewer, say the steps aloud, show them on a made-up example, and let members practice by speaking, pointing or choosing. The member leaves with one small next step they said, not one they wrote.

Most worksheets have the same parts. Each part has a no-writing version:

Worksheet partNo-writing version
Reading the skill stepsYou read the steps aloud. Put no more than three on the board, in large print, one line each.
A written exampleYou act out a fictional example, or describe one in two or three sentences.
Fill in the blanksMembers call out answers and you write them on the board.
A 0 to 10 rating lineMembers say a number, show fingers, or point on the board thermometer.
"Write your plan"Members point to a skill card, or say one small step to the group or to you after group.
Homework sheetAn optional card with one word on it to take home, or nothing at all.

Here is how that sounds in the imagined PHP group. The skill is pausing before acting on an urge. Every line is fictional.

"Today's skill has three steps, and they're on the board: notice, pause, choose. I'll show you first. Say someone named Lee feels the urge to leave a waiting room. Lee notices: 'I want to walk out.' Lee pauses: counts the chairs in the room. Then Lee chooses: stay five more minutes. Which step was counting the chairs? Say it, point to it on the board, or just think it."

Then move to practice with a choice, not an open question. "Here are two things Lee could do next. A, walk out right away. B, take one more pause. Which one uses the skill? Thumbs for A, or hands for B." Finish with the skill cards: "Point to the step you might try before tomorrow."

In the note, record what the member did and the format they used. "Identified the pause step by pointing to the board" is an observed behavior. It is as documentable as a completed worksheet.

Reading the room on a sedated day

On a low-energy day, lower the demand before the room has to ask. Shorter rounds, more pointing, a stand-or-stay stretch and no pressure to speak will usually keep more members in the session than the original plan.

The signs are easy to spot: slow answers, heavy eyes, members losing the thread halfway through an instruction. Adjust without announcing it:

  • Shorten everything. One-word rounds instead of sentences. One example instead of two.
  • One instruction at a time. Give it, repeat it once, then wait.
  • Fewer choices. Two or three cards, not eight.
  • Add movement with an opt-out. A stand-or-stay stretch between parts of the lesson.
  • Swap in a gentle grounding start. Light, eyes-open tasks work better than anything that feels like a test. See 20 eyes-open grounding exercises for group therapy.

Don't name the room's energy as a problem. "You all seem out of it today" puts members on the spot. "Let's keep things short today" does the same work without judging anyone.

Watch for one member who is different from the rest. If someone seems much drowsier, more confused or harder to rouse than is usual for them, that is an observation to pass on, not something to work around in the activity. Follow your program's procedures, and if you are leading alone, request support through your program's route without leaving the group unattended.

If low-energy days are common in your schedule, plan for them. Keep a few no-writing sessions in your rotation. For topic ideas that fit adult programs, see 78 IOP and PHP group topics.

Common questions

What group activities work for adults with serious mental illness who can't do worksheets?

Use formats that need no reading or writing. Spoken rounds with a set length, such as one word or one number, work well. So do point-to choices on a board, either/or questions, and simple drawn marks like a line or a color. Offer the no-writing route to the whole room as the default, and keep a real right to pass.

How do I teach a skills group without handouts?

Teach one skill with no more than three steps. Say the steps aloud and put them on the board in large print. Act out a made-up example instead of asking members to read one. Let members practice by saying, pointing or choosing, and you write their answers on the board. End with one small next step said aloud, not written.

What should I do when most of the group seems sedated or foggy?

Lower the demand before members have to ask. Shorten every round, give one instruction at a time and repeat it once, cut the choices to two or three, and add a stand-or-stay stretch. Don't name the room's energy as a problem. If one member seems much drowsier or more confused than usual for them, pass it on and follow your program's procedures.

Is it OK to let group members skip the writing part of an activity?

In most groups, yes. The writing is usually a route to the skill, not the skill itself, so a member who answers by speaking or pointing has still practiced. Some programs require certain forms to be completed, so follow your program's policy for those. In your note, record the format the member used, such as answered by pointing.
